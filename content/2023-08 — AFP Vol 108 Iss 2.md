## Q1 — Prenatal Care: An Evidence-Based Approach (p. 139)
A pregnant patient at 37 weeks who has had guideline-recommended prenatal care and is RhD-negative. Which action is correct?

- A. Administer Rho(D) immune globulin at this visit.
- B. Administer Rho(D) immune globulin at 38 weeks' gestation.
- C. After delivery, administer Rho(D) immune globulin because of her RhD-negative status.
- D. After delivery, administer Rho(D) immune globulin if the infant is RhD-positive.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — After delivery, give Rho(D) immune globulin if the infant is RhD-positive.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> She already received routine antenatal anti-D at 28 weeks. The postpartum dose is given **only if the newborn is RhD-positive** — there's no benefit in sensitizing-risk terms to dosing an RhD-negative infant's mother when the baby is RhD-negative.

## Q2 — Prenatal Care (p. 139)
A 28-year-old at 14 weeks with COVID-19, minimal URI symptoms, normal O₂ saturation. Which statement is correct?

- A. The patient should not receive a COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy.
- B. The patient should be hospitalized and receive aggressive hydration.
- C. The patient should be screened for gestational diabetes between 12 and 16 weeks.
- D. The patient should be started on low-dose aspirin, 81 mg daily.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Start low-dose aspirin, 81 mg daily.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> COVID-19 in pregnancy is treated as a risk factor for preeclampsia, so low-dose aspirin for preeclampsia prevention is appropriate. COVID-19 vaccination **is** recommended in pregnancy (A wrong); mild infection doesn't warrant hospitalization (B); and infection isn't an indication for early GDM screening (C).

## Q3 — Joint and Soft Tissue Injections (p. 151)
For which condition does evidence support platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections?

- A. Common extensor tendinopathy.
- B. Achilles tendinopathy.
- C. Patellar tendinopathy.
- D. Rotator cuff tendinopathy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Common extensor tendinopathy (lateral epicondylitis).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The best (though still mixed) PRP evidence is for lateral epicondylitis. Data for Achilles, patellar, and rotator cuff tendinopathy are insufficient or negative.

## Q4 — Joint and Soft Tissue Injections (p. 151)
Which statement about corticosteroid injections is correct?

- A. High-quality evidence supports their use for adhesive capsulitis, de Quervain tenosynovitis, and trigger finger.
- B. They are superior to rehabilitation for greater trochanteric pain and common extensor tendinopathy.
- C. Combined with rehabilitation, they give greater relief than rehabilitation alone for knee osteoarthritis.
- D. They decreased cartilage thickness over five years for symptomatic knee OA flare-ups.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — High-quality evidence supports use for adhesive capsulitis, de Quervain tenosynovitis, and trigger finger.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> These three respond well to corticosteroid injection. For lateral epicondylitis and GTPS, steroids give short-term relief but are **inferior** long-term to rehab (B wrong); the knee-OA cartilage-loss signal was over ~2 years, not 5 (D wrong).

## Q5 — Food Allergies (p. 159)
Parent worried a 3-year-old may be allergic to peanuts. First step in establishing a food-allergy diagnosis?

- A. A skin prick test.
- B. A detailed allergy-focused history.
- C. A serum IgE test.
- D. An oral food challenge.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — A detailed allergy-focused history.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> History guides everything: it determines whether and which testing is appropriate. Skin prick and serum IgE confirm sensitization but have poor specificity in isolation; the oral food challenge is the gold standard but is reserved for after history ± testing.

## Q6 — Food Allergies (p. 159)
A 25-year-old with a convincing history of urticarial reactions to peanuts. Most appropriate **initial** counseling?

- A. Learn to interpret food labels and avoid peanuts in the diet.
- B. Start daily oral antihistamines and continue eating peanuts.
- C. Start sublingual immunotherapy and continue eating peanuts.
- D. Use an epinephrine injector before eating peanuts.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Learn to interpret food labels and avoid peanuts.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Strict allergen avoidance plus label-reading education is the cornerstone of food-allergy management. Patients also carry epinephrine **for accidental exposure** — not to pre-medicate before deliberately eating the allergen.

## Q7 — Pressure Injuries (p. 166)
Which patient is at risk of pressure injuries?

- A. A 4-day-old infant born at 29 weeks receiving supplemental oxygen in the NICU.
- B. A 78-year-old woman in rehab after total hip arthroplasty.
- C. A 46-year-old man with morbid obesity, limited mobility, and type 2 diabetes living at home.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Pressure injuries are not limited to elderly inpatients — neonates (device-related), post-surgical/immobilized older adults, and immobile community-dwelling adults with diabetes are all at risk.

## Q8 — Pressure Injuries (p. 166)
Which is a key **evidence-based** component to prevent pressure injuries in hospital?

- A. Standardized repositioning and mobilization plans.
- B. Using alkaline soaps for cleansing.
- C. Limiting patient-initiated position changes.
- D. Using high-specification foam mattresses vs. regular foam for people at increased risk.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — High-specification foam mattresses vs. regular foam for at-risk patients.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Cochrane evidence supports high-specification (reactive) foam mattresses over standard foam. Repositioning is widely practiced but its evidence is weaker; alkaline soaps harm skin; and restricting patient movement is counterproductive.

## Q9 — Evaluation of Acute Pelvic Pain in Women (p. 175)
A 22-year-old sexually active woman with 2 days of acute pelvic pain. Which statement about lab testing is correct?

- A. A urine pregnancy test is typically positive within 24 hours of implantation.
- B. Only one-half of pregnancies can be detected by 7 days after the expected menstrual period.
- C. CBC, ESR, and CRP are recommended as initial screening for acute pelvic pain.
- D. A first-void urine sample is recommended to test for gonorrhea and chlamydia.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — A first-void (first-catch) urine sample for gonorrhea and chlamydia NAAT.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> First-catch urine NAAT is a validated, noninvasive way to test for GC/CT. Pregnancy tests aren't reliably positive within 24 h of implantation, and inflammatory markers aren't recommended as routine initial screening for undifferentiated pelvic pain.

## Q10 — Evaluation of Acute Pelvic Pain (p. 175)
Which statement about imaging for women with acute pelvic pain is correct?

- A. CT is the recommended initial test to rule out ectopic pregnancy.
- B. CT produces excellent soft tissue images of the reproductive organs.
- C. CT has low sensitivity for diagnosing appendicitis.
- D. CT is the recommended initial test for suspected urinary etiologies.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — CT is the recommended initial test for suspected urinary etiologies.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> For suspected urinary causes (e.g., stones), CT is the initial study. **Transvaginal ultrasound** — not CT — is first-line for ectopic and reproductive-organ pathology; CT actually has **high** sensitivity for appendicitis.

## Q11 — Speech and Language Delay in Children (p. 181)
A 2-year-old says only a few words, doesn't combine them, bilingual (Spanish/English) home, shy at exam, otherwise normal development. Most appropriate next step?

- A. Administer a validated screening tool.
- B. Follow up at the 3-year visit because of the bilingual household.
- C. Note the concern and follow up at 30 months.
- D. Refer to pediatric neurology and developmental pediatrics.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Administer a validated screening tool.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A bilingual child should have a comparable **total** vocabulary across both languages by age 2; "only a few words in each language" is a red flag, not something to attribute to bilingualism. Screen objectively before deciding on referral — don't defer (B, C) and don't jump straight to subspecialty referral (D).

## Q12 — Speech and Language Delay (p. 181)
A 4-year-old referred to audiology (normal) and speech-language pathology, but SLP evaluation is delayed ≥4 months by insurance. Which alternative evaluation source can you recommend?

- A. Early intervention programs run by the government.
- B. Physical therapy.
- C. Public elementary school for school-system evaluation.
- D. Occupational therapy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Public elementary (school-system) evaluation.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Government **early intervention** serves children **under 3**; at age 4 the **public school system** is responsible for developmental/speech evaluation. (Key age cut-off: <3 → early intervention; ≥3 → school district.)

## Q13 — Cochrane: Inspiratory Muscle Training for COPD (p. 136)
A COPD patient who can't attend pulmonary rehab; you suggest inspiratory muscle training. Which statement is correct?

- A. It uses targeted exercises/devices to increase strength and endurance of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
- B. When combined with pulmonary rehabilitation, it makes no difference.
- C. When used alone, it can improve symptoms, physical fitness, and quality of life.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The Cochrane review supports each statement: IMT is device-based respiratory-muscle training (A); used alone it improves dyspnea, fitness, and QoL (C); but **added to** pulmonary rehabilitation it provides no clear incremental benefit (B). Hence "all of the above."

## Q14 — Practice Guidelines: Bowel Injury in Trauma (WSES) (p. 206)
A 38-year-old after an MVC: normal vitals, seat-belt bruising but no peritoneal signs, US shows no free fluid, CT shows no definite bowel injury. Per WSES, most appropriate next step?

- A. Serial abdominal examinations.
- B. Laparoscopy.
- C. Laparotomy.
- D. Peritoneal lavage.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Serial abdominal examinations.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A hemodynamically stable patient with a seat-belt sign but no definite injury on imaging is observed with **serial exams** (the seat-belt sign raises suspicion for delayed hollow-viscus injury). Operative intervention is reserved for instability or clear/evolving injury; diagnostic peritoneal lavage is largely obsolete.

## Q15 — Practice Guidelines: Perioperative Antithrombotics (ACCP) (p. 208)
A 45-year-old on clopidogrel + aspirin for a cardiac stent placed **4 weeks ago**, scheduled for elective symptomatic hernia repair (moderate-risk). Per ACCP, which is appropriate?

- A. Delay the surgery.
- B. Hold clopidogrel 5 days before surgery, continue aspirin.
- C. Hold aspirin 7 days before surgery, continue clopidogrel.
- D. Hold aspirin 7 days before and clopidogrel 5 days before surgery.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Delay the surgery.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Dual antiplatelet therapy should not be interrupted this soon after stent placement (very high stent-thrombosis risk in the first weeks–months). For an **elective** procedure, the correct move is to **postpone surgery** until the mandatory DAPT period has elapsed.
