## Q1 — Acute Hand Infections (p. 228)

Which one of the following statements about the management of hand infections is correct?

- A. Wound irrigation with povidone-iodine solution is more beneficial than saline alone.
- B. Patients who received a tetanus toxoid–containing vaccine in the prior five years do not require another dose.
- C. Splints increase the risk of flexion contractures.
- D. Irrigation of open wounds with tap water is contraindicated.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — A tetanus-containing vaccine within the prior five years means no additional dose is needed.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Povidone-iodine offers no advantage (and is tissue-toxic) over **saline/tap water** (A, D wrong); **splinting in the safe position prevents** contractures (C reversed).

## Q2 — Acute Hand Infections (p. 228)

Which one of the following statements about the treatment of acute paronychia is correct?

- A. Oral antibiotics should be administered for five days after surgical drainage.
- B. Warm soaks and topical antibiotics are sufficient treatment for patients with no evidence of abscess.
- C. Wound cultures should be obtained from patients with an abscess.
- D. Pseudomonas is the most common pathogen.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Without an abscess, warm soaks and topical antibiotics suffice.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> An abscess needs **incision and drainage** (often without routine culture, C); oral antibiotics aren't automatically needed after drainage (A); **Staphylococcus aureus**, not Pseudomonas, is the usual pathogen (D).

## Q3 — Acute Hand Infections (p. 228)

Which one of the following is recommended for the management of herpetic whitlow?

- A. Incision and drainage.
- B. Parenteral antibiotics.
- C. Topical antibiotics.
- D. Antiviral medication in immunocompromised patients.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Antiviral medication in immunocompromised patients.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Herpetic whitlow is **viral and self-limited** — **do not incise and drain** (risk of bacterial superinfection/spread, A). Antibiotics don't treat it (B, C); antivirals are reserved for severe/recurrent or immunocompromised cases.

## Q4 — Type 2 Diabetes Therapies: A STEPS Approach (p. 237)

Which one of the following medications has good evidence that it improves patient-oriented outcomes?

- A. Glyburide.
- B. Acarbose (Precose).
- C. Rosiglitazone (Avandia).
- D. Pramlintide (Symlin).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Acarbose.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Acarbose has trial evidence (e.g., STOP-NIDDM) for patient-oriented/cardiovascular benefit. Glyburide causes hypoglycemia, **rosiglitazone** carries cardiovascular concerns, and pramlintide lacks outcome data (A, C, D).

## Q5 — Type 2 Diabetes Therapies (p. 237)

Which one of the following medications commonly causes prominent gastrointestinal adverse effects?

- A. Acarbose.
- B. Glipizide (Glucotrol).
- C. Pioglitazone (Actos).
- D. Glargine insulin (Lantus).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Acarbose.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors cause prominent **flatulence, bloating, and diarrhea** from carbohydrate malabsorption — the main limit on their use.

## Q6 — Type 2 Diabetes Therapies (p. 237)

Which one of the following medications is **not** associated with a significant risk of hypoglycemia?

- A. Pramlintide.
- B. Glyburide.
- C. Insulin.
- D. Metformin (Glucophage).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Metformin.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Metformin doesn't cause hypoglycemia as monotherapy. **Sulfonylureas (glyburide), insulin, and pramlintide** (with insulin) carry meaningful hypoglycemia risk (A, B, C).

## Q7 — Manipulative Therapies: What Works (p. 248)

Which one of the following statements about spinal manipulation for patients with acute low back pain is correct?

- A. It is more effective than physical therapy, analgesics, or back school.
- B. It is similarly effective to physical therapy, analgesics, or back school.
- C. It is less effective than physical therapy, analgesics, or back school.
- D. It has not been studied in patients with acute low back pain.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Similarly effective to physical therapy, analgesics, or back school.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Spinal manipulation gives **comparable** (modest) benefit to other standard conservative options for acute low back pain — neither clearly superior nor inferior.

## Q8 — Manipulative Therapies (p. 248)

Which one of the following statements about spinal manipulation for infantile colic, respiratory conditions, or otitis media in children and infants is correct?

- A. It has been shown to be somewhat effective for these conditions.
- B. It has not been shown to be effective for these conditions.
- C. It has been shown to be harmful for these conditions.
- D. It has not been studied in children or infants.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — It has not been shown to be effective for these conditions.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Evidence does not support spinal manipulation for colic, asthma/respiratory illness, or otitis media in children.

## Q9 — Manipulative Therapies (p. 248)

Which one of the following statements about spinal manipulation and/or mobilization for patients with neck pain is correct?

- A. It is more effective than no treatment for short-term relief of chronic neck pain when performed in combination with exercise.
- B. It is less effective than oral analgesics for acute or subacute neck pain.
- C. It is not effective for chronic neck pain, with or without exercise.
- D. It has not been studied for this indication.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Combined with exercise, it provides short-term relief of chronic neck pain vs. no treatment.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Manipulation/mobilization **plus exercise** offers short-term chronic neck-pain benefit (so C is wrong), and it has been studied (D wrong).

## Q10 — Putting Prevention into Practice: Screening for Cervical Cancer (p. 253)

A 48-year-old woman had a negative HPV test and Pap smear three years ago. She asks about a Pap today, and about screening for her newly sexually active 19-year-old daughter. According to the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, how should you respond?

- A. You should perform a Pap smear at this appointment and schedule a Pap smear for her daughter next week.
- B. You should suggest that she have an HPV test and that her daughter does the same.
- C. You should tell her that she can wait another two years and get an HPV test alone or in combination with the Pap smear and that her daughter should have her first Pap smear at 21 years of age.
- D. You should tell her that she should get her Pap smear today and that her daughter should get an HPV test soon.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — She can wait (5-year HPV ± cytology interval); her daughter starts screening at age 21.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> For ages 30–65, options include **cytology every 3 years, HPV every 5 years, or co-testing every 5 years** — so after a negative co-test she's due in ~2 more years. **Screening begins at 21** regardless of sexual activity, so the 19-year-old isn't screened yet.

## Q11 — Practice Guidelines: ACIP Approves 2019 Adult and Childhood/Adolescent Immunization Schedules (p. 264)

Which one of the following statements is most consistent with the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) 2019 recommendations?

- A. Quadrivalent live attenuated influenza vaccine should be preferentially given this influenza season to children who do not have contraindications.
- B. Hepatitis A vaccine is recommended in persons experiencing homelessness.
- C. HepB-CpG may not be interchanged with hepatitis B vaccines from other manufacturers even if it is not available to complete the series.
- D. HepB-CpG is recommended for use in pregnant and nonpregnant people older than 18 years.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Hepatitis A vaccine is recommended for persons experiencing homelessness.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> ACIP added homelessness as a hepatitis A indication (outbreak-driven). LAIV is **not preferentially** recommended (no preference among flu vaccines, A); other HepB vaccines **can complete a HepB-CpG series** if needed (C wrong); HepB-CpG isn't recommended **in pregnancy** due to insufficient data (D wrong).
