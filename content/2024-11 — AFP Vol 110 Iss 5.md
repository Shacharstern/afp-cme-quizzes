## Q1 — Common Tongue Conditions in Primary Care (p. 467)
Which one of the following tongue conditions is potentially malignant?

- A. Black hairy tongue.
- B. Erythroplakia.
- C. Geographic tongue.
- D. Median rhomboid glossitis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Erythroplakia.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Erythroplakia is a red, velvety mucosal lesion with high malignant potential (frequently shows dysplasia/carcinoma on biopsy). Black hairy tongue, geographic tongue, and median rhomboid glossitis are all benign.

## Q2 — Common Tongue Conditions (p. 467)

- A. It can be easily scraped off the tongue.
- B. The rate of progression to oral cancer is about 10% per year.
- C. Older age is a risk factor for progression to cancer.
- D. Smoking is not a known risk factor.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Older age is a risk factor for progression to cancer.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High (Verified)
> Leukoplakia is a white plaque that **cannot** be scraped off (distinguishing it from candidiasis — A wrong). Malignant transformation is roughly 1% per year, not 10% (B wrong). Smoking is a major risk factor (D wrong). Older age, large size, and non-homogeneous/ventral-tongue lesions raise transformation risk.

## Q4 — Diabetic Ketoacidosis (p. 476)
Which one of the following statements about the management of DKA in children is correct?

- A. In the absence of shock, 1 L of 0.9% normal saline should be administered over 15 minutes.
- B. Administer insulin at 0.05 to 0.1 unit/kg per hour if no hypokalemia is present, beginning 1 to 2 hours after fluid initiation.
- C. Glucose should not be added to intravenous fluids.
- D. Chest radiography and urine and blood cultures should be ordered routinely.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Insulin 0.05–0.1 unit/kg/hr (no bolus) starting 1–2 h after fluids.** 🧠 Generated (Verified) · Confidence: High
> In children, fluids are given more cautiously (10–20 mL/kg over 1–2 h, not 1 L over 15 min) to limit cerebral edema risk (A wrong); insulin is started 1–2 h after fluids without a bolus. Dextrose IS added once glucose falls (C wrong), and infectious workup is guided by clinical suspicion, not routine (D wrong).

## Q5 — Genital Herpes: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 487)

- B. Condoms do not help prevent HSV transmission.
- C. Once-daily valacyclovir can reduce the risk of infection for serodiscordant partners.
- D. HSV is rarely transmitted vertically to infants by a pregnant patient; therefore, prophylaxis is not recommended.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Daily valacyclovir reduces transmission risk to serodiscordant partners.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High

Vertical transmission causes serious neonatal disease, so late-pregnancy suppressive therapy is recommended (D wrong). Suppressive valacyclovir lowers transmission to uninfected partners.

## Q6 — Genital Herpes (p. 487)

> [!success]- Reveal answer

## Q9 — Bleeding and Bruising: Primary Care Evaluation (p. 504)
An older patient bruises easily and has frequent nosebleeds. Which one of the following is most suggestive of an underlying bleeding disorder?

- A. Thinning of the skin.
- B. Bruising secondary to injectable medication.
- C. An ISTH-BAT score of 1 to 3.
- D. Bleeding from two or more mucocutaneous sites.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Bleeding from two or more mucocutaneous sites.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Multiple-site mucocutaneous bleeding suggests a true hemostatic disorder. Skin thinning (senile/steroid purpura) and injection-site bruising are local/benign. A low ISTH-BAT score (1–3) is within the normal range and reassuring, not concerning.

## Q10 — Bleeding and Bruising (p. 504)
Family history of easy bruising, heavy periods, numerous nosebleeds; concerning ISTH-BAT score but normal PT and aPTT. Best approach to evaluation?

- A. Using the TEN-4-FACESp clinical decision rule.
- B. Evaluation of bleeding time.
- C. Early consideration of von Willebrand disease.
- D. Thromboelastography.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Early consideration of von Willebrand disease.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> vWD is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, classically presents with mucocutaneous/menorrhagic bleeding and a positive family history, and frequently has **normal** PT/aPTT — so normal coagulation times don't exclude it. TEN-4-FACESp screens for abusive bruising in children; bleeding time and thromboelastography are not standard first-line workup.

## Q12 — Rheumatoid Arthritis (p. 515)
Which one of the following agents is a first-line disease-modifying treatment for RA?

- A. NSAID at full dosage.
- B. Glucocorticoids.
- C. Janus kinase inhibitors.
- D. Methotrexate.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Methotrexate.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High

## Q13 — Cochrane: Testosterone Replacement Therapy in Men With Sexual Dysfunction (p. 464)
A man reports sexual dysfunction and requests TRT. Which one of the following statements about TRT for men with an unknown testosterone level is correct?

- A. It improves sexual quality of life.
- B. It improves symptoms of erectile function.
- C. It is associated with little to no difference in cardiovascular mortality, withdrawal due to adverse events, prostate-related events, and lower urinary tract symptoms over 12 months.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — It is associated with little to no difference in cardiovascular mortality, withdrawal due to adverse events, prostate-related events, and lower urinary tract symptoms over 12 months.** 🧠 Generated (Corrected) · Confidence: High 
> The 2024 Cochrane systematic review on testosterone replacement therapy in men with sexual dysfunction found that *TRT was associated with little to no difference in several key safety outcomes over 12 months*.[1] Specifically:
*Cardiovascular mortality*: The TRAVERSE trial, which followed 5,198 men for a mean of 22 months, demonstrated that testosterone-replacement therapy was noninferior to placebo with respect to major adverse cardiac events (hazard ratio 0.96, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.17). Testosterone treatment has not been reported to increase the risk of MACE.[2][3]
•⁠  ⁠*Withdrawal due to adverse events*: Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found similar rates of study withdrawal between testosterone and placebo groups.[4]
•⁠  ⁠*Prostate-related events*: Trials have not shown that testosterone treatment increases the risk of prostate cancer or lower urinary tract symptoms. The TRAVERSE prostate safety substudy showed no significant difference in high-grade prostate cancer, any prostate cancer, acute urinary retention, or invasive procedures for benign prostatic hyperplasia between testosterone and placebo groups over 4 years.[3][5]
•⁠  ⁠*Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS): The Testosterone Trials observed that during the year of treatment, the International Prostate Symptom Score increased to >19 (indicating moderately severe LUTS) in **similar numbers* of men in the placebo and testosterone arms. A 2018 meta-analysis found TRT "had no significant effect on lower urinary tract symptoms".[6][7]

A 38-year-old asymptomatic woman, 15 pack-years, asks about COPD screening. Per the USPSTF, how should she be counseled?

- B. Screening would benefit her because there is no harm in screening.

## Q16 — Practice Guidelines: Anaphylaxis (Joint Task Force) (p. 544)
A 10-month-old on an airplane has lip swelling, vomiting ×2, and generalized rash after kiwi, resolving completely 10 minutes after a 0.15-mg epinephrine autoinjector. Per the JTF, the most appropriate next step is which one of the following?

- A. Land the plane at the closest location and activate EMS.
- B. Administer a second epinephrine autoinjector to prevent recurrent anaphylaxis.
- C. Monitor for at least 1 hour.
- D. Transfer the patient to EMS after landing at the original destination.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Monitor for at least 1 hour.** 🧠 Generated (Verified) · Confidence: High 
> The 2023 JTF parameters reduced mandatory observation: patients with one or two risk factors for severe/biphasic reactions should be observed ~1 hour after symptom resolution. Complete resolution after a single dose argues against an immediate second dose (B) and against emergency diversion (A).
