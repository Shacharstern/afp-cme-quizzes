## Q1 — Hair Loss: Diagnosis and Treatment (p. 243)
A 30-year-old woman 2 months postpartum has diffuse hair loss across the scalp with clumps in her brush. Most likely cause?

- A. Anagen effluvium.
- B. Female pattern androgenetic alopecia.
- C. Alopecia areata.
- D. Telogen effluvium.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Telogen effluvium.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Postpartum diffuse shedding 2–4 months after a physiologic stressor (delivery) is classic telogen effluvium: hairs synchronously shift into telogen and shed. Anagen effluvium is chemo-related, androgenetic alopecia is patterned/gradual, and alopecia areata produces discrete bald patches.

## Q2 — Hair Loss (p. 243)
A 50-year-old man has frontal-temporal and now vertex thinning. Most effective treatment for this condition?

- A. Finasteride, 1 mg daily.
- B. Topical high-potency corticosteroid.
- C. Intralesional corticosteroids.
- D. Oral baricitinib (Olumiant).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Finasteride 1 mg daily.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> This is male androgenetic alopecia. Oral finasteride (5α-reductase inhibitor) is the most effective evidence-based therapy (minoxidil is the topical mainstay). Corticosteroids treat alopecia areata, and baricitinib is a JAK inhibitor for severe alopecia areata — not androgenetic alopecia.

## Q3 — Urinary Catheter Management (p. 251)
Which one of the following complications is most often associated with clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) and may require intervention?

- A. Bladder spasms.
- B. Skin breakdown.
- C. Creation of false passages.
- D. Restricted activities of daily living.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Creation of false passages.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Repeated self-catheterization can cause urethral trauma and false passages, which may require intervention (e.g., temporary indwelling catheter, urology). Bladder spasms and skin breakdown are more typical of indwelling catheters. **⚠️ Verify** (UTI is the most common overall CIC complication but isn't listed).

## Q4 — Urinary Catheter Management (p. 251)
Which one of the following statements about long-term catheters is consistent with current urologic recommendations?

- A. Long-term catheters should not be left in place longer than 2 weeks before replacement.
- B. Empiric antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended for all patients undergoing long-term catheter replacement.
- C. Timing of long-term catheter replacement should be individualized rather than based on a fixed schedule.
- D. Screening urine cultures are routinely recommended during long-term catheter use to monitor for infection.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Replacement timing should be individualized, not on a fixed schedule.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Routine antibiotic prophylaxis at exchange is not recommended, and asymptomatic bacteriuria/colonization is universal — so screening cultures of an asymptomatic catheterized patient should not be done (B, D wrong). There is no mandatory 2-week interval (A wrong); replacement is individualized to encrustation/blockage patterns.

## Q5 — Palpitations: Evaluation, Management, and Wearable Smart Devices (p. 259)
A 65-year-old with anxiety has occasional chest fluttering at his desk; his smartphone flagged an irregular heartbeat. History, exam, and resting ECG are normal. Most appropriate next step?

- A. Provide reassurance that the experience is benign.
- B. Recommend ambulatory ECG monitoring.
- C. Order cardiac stress testing.
- D. Perform an electrophysiologic study.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Ambulatory ECG monitoring.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Intermittent palpitations with a wearable-device alert and a normal resting ECG warrant ambulatory rhythm monitoring to capture the arrhythmia during symptoms (rule in/out paroxysmal AF). Reassurance without documenting the rhythm is premature; stress testing and EP study aren't first-line here.

## Q6 — Palpitations (p. 259)
A 43-year-old with intermittent rapid heartbeat. Which one of the following findings warrants immediate cardiology evaluation?

- A. History of panic attacks.
- B. Presence of symptoms while exercising.
- C. Premature atrial beats on ECG.
- D. Premature ventricular beats on ECG.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Palpitations occurring during exertion.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Exertional palpitations (or syncope) are a red flag for structural heart disease or a malignant arrhythmia and warrant prompt cardiology evaluation. Panic-attack history, isolated PACs, and occasional PVCs are generally benign.

## Q7 — Cushing's Syndrome: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 270)
In which one of the following patients could a dexamethasone suppression test (DST) cause a false-positive result for hypercortisolism?

- A. A patient with an adrenal tumor.
- B. A patient who works the night shift.
- C. A pregnant patient.
- D. A patient with a disrupted circadian rhythm.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — A pregnant patient.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Pregnancy (and estrogen use) raises cortisol-binding globulin and produces physiologic hypercortisolism, causing a false-positive DST. An adrenal tumor would be a true positive. Shift work/disrupted circadian rhythm affects late-night salivary cortisol rather than the DST. 

## Q8 — Cushing's Syndrome (p. 270)
Which one of the following statements about the prognosis for patients with Cushing's syndrome is correct?

- A. Lifelong monitoring is necessary.
- B. Mortality is substantially improved after treatment.
- C. Recurrence rates range between 1% and 4%.
- D. Recurrence usually happens within the first year after surgery.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Lifelong monitoring is necessary.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Recurrence can occur years after apparently curative surgery, and cardiometabolic/comorbidity risk persists, so lifelong surveillance is required. Recurrence rates are higher than 1–4% and are not confined to the first year; mortality, while improved, remains elevated above the general population.

## Q10 — Alzheimer Disease (p. 281)
Which one of the following statements about the evidence for vitamin E in Alzheimer disease is correct?

- A. A Cochrane review found no evidence that vitamin E improves cognition or slows progression of MCI or dementia.
- B. The optimal dosage of vitamin E in patients with Alzheimer disease is 200 IU per day.
- C. Vitamin E is contraindicated in patients taking cholinesterase inhibitors.
- D. There is no identified toxic dose of vitamin E.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Cochrane found no evidence vitamin E improves cognition or slows MCI/dementia progression.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The Cochrane review showed no cognitive benefit. Studied doses were ~2,000 IU/day, not 200 (B wrong); vitamin E is not contraindicated with cholinesterase inhibitors (C wrong); high-dose vitamin E has recognized harms (bleeding, possible increased mortality), so D is wrong.

## Q13 — Putting Prevention Into Practice: Screening for Lipid Disorders in Children and Adolescents (p. 295)
An 11-year-old boy (BMI 75th percentile, otherwise well). Per the USPSTF, which one of the following answers about the benefits/harms of lipid screening and treatment is correct?

- A. Screening can accurately detect high cholesterol in children.
- B. In children diagnosed with familial hypercholesterolemia, statins can substantially lower cholesterol levels in the short term.
- C. Identifying and treating high cholesterol in children will prevent heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular death in adulthood.
- D. The harms of screening and treatment are substantial.
- E. Treatment with statins can affect normal development and puberty.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — In familial hypercholesterolemia, statins substantially lower cholesterol short-term.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> The USPSTF issued an "I" statement (insufficient evidence) because, although statins clearly lower LDL short-term in FH, there is **no** evidence that childhood screening/treatment prevents adult cardiovascular events (C wrong) and no established substantial harms or effects on puberty (D, E wrong).

## Q14 — Lown Right Care: Routine Vitamin D Testing — Unnecessary and Ineffective (p. 302)
Which one of the following statements about vitamin D screening and supplementation is correct?

- A. The USPSTF recommends screening for vitamin D deficiency.
- B. Daily supplementation with 600 to 800 IU of vitamin D is sufficient to prevent deficiency for most people.
- C. Randomized studies demonstrate that correcting vitamin D deficiency prevents fractures and falls.
- D. High-dose vitamin D supplements are not associated with significant adverse effects.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — 600–800 IU/day prevents deficiency for most people.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The USPSTF does not recommend population screening for vitamin D deficiency (A wrong). RCTs have not shown that correcting levels prevents fractures/falls in community-dwelling adults (C wrong), and high-dose supplementation carries harms such as hypercalcemia and possibly increased falls (D wrong).

## Q15 — Point-of-Care Guides: PREVENT Equations for Assessing Cardiovascular Risk (p. 305)
Which one of the following statements about the PREVENT equations is correct?

- A. They were developed from a multinational registry.
- B. They include social deprivation estimated by a patient's household income.
- C. They predict the 20-year risk of a cerebrovascular event.
- D. They predict the 10-year risk of heart failure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — They predict 10-year (and 30-year) risk of heart failure and ASCVD.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The AHA PREVENT equations were derived from large US cohorts (not a multinational registry), and notably add heart failure to total cardiovascular disease risk, estimating both 10- and 30-year risk. Social deprivation is incorporated via an area-based index (not household income). 

## Q16 — Practice Guidelines: Management of Chronic Coronary Disease (ACC/AHA) (p. 315)
A 62-year-old woman with ACS and PCI in the past 2 years, well-controlled hypertension, no diabetes, EF 55%. Per ACC/AHA, which one of the following medications is most likely to reduce her risk of future coronary events?

- A. Beta blocker.
- B. Aspirin.
- C. Calcium channel blocker.
- D. Long-acting nitrates.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Aspirin.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> In chronic coronary disease, low-dose aspirin reduces future ischemic events (Class 1 secondary prevention). The 2023 guideline no longer mandates long-term beta-blockers in patients with normal EF and remote MI when there's no other indication. Calcium channel blockers and nitrates relieve angina but don't reduce event risk.
