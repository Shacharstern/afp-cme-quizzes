## Q1 — Urinary Tract Infections in Young Children and Infants (p. 278)

In which one of the following patients should a urine specimen be obtained to test for UTI?

- A. A 28-month-old acutely ill boy who is verbal, circumcised, and has no signs or symptoms of UTI.
- B. A 30-month-old acutely ill girl who is verbal and has no signs or symptoms of UTI.
- C. A 10-month-old acutely ill febrile girl whose UTICalc score is less than 1%.
- D. A seven-week-old acutely ill but afebrile girl with no localized urinary tract signs or symptoms.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — The seven-week-old acutely ill infant.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> **Young infants (<2–3 months)** with acute illness need a urine evaluation even when afebrile and without localizing signs, because UTI presents nonspecifically. Older, verbal children without symptoms and a low UTICalc don't require testing (A, B, C).

## Q2 — Urinary Tract Infections in Young Children (p. 278)

Which one of the following statements about the treatment of UTIs in children is correct?

- A. Intravenous antibiotics are required for children with pyelonephritis.
- B. Children should be transitioned to oral antibiotics after they have completed a full 10- to 14-day course of intravenous antibiotics.
- C. A two- to four-day course of antibiotics is indicated for children with cystitis.
- D. Delayed antibiotic treatment is as effective as immediate treatment for prevention of renal scarring in children with UTI.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — A 2–4-day antibiotic course for pediatric cystitis.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Short courses suffice for cystitis. Most pyelonephritis can be treated **orally** if the child tolerates it (A, B wrong); **prompt** treatment reduces renal scarring — delay is not equivalent (D wrong).

## Q3 — Urinary Tract Infections in Young Children (p. 278)

A three-year-old girl is diagnosed with her first uncomplicated UTI. Which one of the following statements about further evaluation and treatment is correct?

- A. Probiotics can prevent recurrences.
- B. Treatment of concurrent bowel and bladder dysfunction will reduce the likelihood of recurrent UTIs.
- C. She should have kidney and bladder ultrasonography within six months.
- D. Imaging should be done with voiding cystourethrography rather than ultrasonography.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Treating bowel and bladder dysfunction reduces recurrent UTIs.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Constipation/dysfunctional voiding strongly drive recurrence — addressing them is high-yield. Probiotics aren't proven (A); **ultrasound is first-line** imaging (VCUG only if abnormal/recurrent), not within a fixed 6-month window (C, D wrong).

## Q4 — Esophageal Motility Disorders (p. 291)

Which one of the following clinical findings most strongly suggests a functional esophageal disorder?

- A. Difficulty swallowing liquids as well as solids.
- B. Dysphagia with cold liquids.
- C. Chest pain unrelated to meals.
- D. Regurgitation of food several hours after a meal.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Chest pain unrelated to meals.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Non-meal-related chest pain (after cardiac causes are excluded) points to a **functional** esophageal disorder. Dysphagia to both liquids and solids suggests a motility disorder (A); food regurgitation hours later suggests achalasia/obstruction (D).

## Q5 — Esophageal Motility Disorders (p. 291)

Which one of the following medications should be tried before other interventions for a patient who has esophageal pain with a suspected benign origin?

- A. Opioids.
- B. Acid-suppressing therapy.
- C. Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor.
- D. Calcium channel blocker.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Acid-suppressing therapy (a PPI trial).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> An empiric **PPI** trial is first-line because GERD commonly mimics esophageal pain/dysmotility. Opioids worsen dysmotility (A); CCBs/PDE-5 inhibitors are reserved for confirmed spastic disorders (C, D).

## Q6 — Esophageal Motility Disorders (p. 291)

Which one of the following statements about interventions for esophageal motility disorders is correct?

- A. Achalasia usually responds well to medical therapy.
- B. Empiric treatment of hypercontractile motility disorders with calcium channel blockers, nitrates, or phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors should be tried before the patient undergoes high-resolution manometry.
- C. Many esophageal symptoms improve with mindful eating, smaller meals, and avoiding trigger foods.
- D. Opioid-related dysmotility is generally irreversible.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Many esophageal symptoms improve with mindful eating, smaller meals, and trigger avoidance.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Simple behavioral measures help. Achalasia needs **mechanical/surgical** therapy, not medications (A wrong); confirm the diagnosis with **manometry before** empiric spasmolytics (B wrong); opioid-induced dysmotility often **improves** after stopping the opioid (D wrong).

## Q7 — Thyroid Nodules: Advances in Evaluation and Management (p. 298)

Which one of the following is the preferred initial test for patients with a thyroid nodule?

- A. Radionuclide thyroid uptake scan.
- B. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level.
- C. Thyroid ultrasonography alone.
- D. TSH level plus thyroid ultrasonography.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — TSH level plus thyroid ultrasonography.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Evaluate every thyroid nodule with **both** a TSH and a dedicated thyroid ultrasound — the TSH directs next steps and the ultrasound risk-stratifies for possible FNA.

## Q8 — Thyroid Nodules (p. 298)

Which one of the following is the next preferred test for patients with a thyroid nodule and a low TSH level?

- A. Fine-needle aspiration.
- B. Molecular testing.
- C. Radionuclide thyroid uptake scan.
- D. Diagnostic lobectomy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Radionuclide (radioiodine) uptake scan.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A **low TSH** suggests a possible autonomously functioning ("hot") nodule → get an uptake scan. Hot nodules are rarely malignant and **don't need FNA** (A). FNA/molecular testing apply to non-functioning nodules.

## Q9 — Thyroid Nodules (p. 298)

Which one of the following is the best management option for nonpregnant patients with hyperthyroidism caused by hyperfunctioning benign thyroid nodules?

- A. Fine-needle aspiration.
- B. Radioactive iodine ablation.
- C. Antithyroid drugs.
- D. Active surveillance.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Radioactive iodine ablation.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A toxic (hyperfunctioning) nodule is best treated definitively with **radioactive iodine** (or surgery). Antithyroid drugs only suppress while taken (C); active surveillance leaves the patient hyperthyroid (D); FNA isn't needed for a hot nodule (A).

## Q10 — Cochrane for Clinicians: PPAR Gamma Agonists for Preventing Recurrent Stroke and Vascular Events (p. 271)

Your 60-year-old patient is admitted for ischemic stroke, has an A1C of 7.2%, and is diagnosed with diabetes. Which one of the following statements about the possibility of recurrent stroke is correct?

- A. The recurrence risk is 30%.
- B. PPAR gamma agonists will probably reduce the risk of stroke recurrence.
- C. PPAR gamma agonists are associated with adverse effects including edema, anemia, liver dysfunction, and heart failure.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> In insulin-resistant patients (e.g., IRIS trial with pioglitazone), PPAR-gamma agonists reduce recurrent stroke/MI, but at the cost of edema, weight gain, fractures, and heart-failure risk — so benefits must be weighed against harms.

## Q11 — Cochrane for Clinicians: Corticosteroids for Hospitalized Patients with Community-Acquired Pneumonia (p. 272)

Which one of the following statements about corticosteroid therapy in hospitalized patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is correct?

- A. In an adult with severe CAP (PSI score greater than 4), it may reduce mortality.
- B. In an adult with mild CAP (PSI score less than 4), it reduces mortality.
- C. In a child with severe CAP, it does not reduce the risk of clinical failure.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — In adults with severe CAP, corticosteroids may reduce mortality.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The mortality benefit is seen in **severe** CAP, not mild (B wrong). In children, steroids **reduce** clinical failure (C wrong), so "all of the above" is incorrect (D).

## Q12 — Practice Guidelines: Treatment of Subclinical Hypothyroidism — BMJ Rapid Recommendation (p. 316)

A 69-year-old with constipation, depression, and fatigue has a TSH of 16.6 mIU/L and a normal free thyroxine level. Which one of the following benefits should be expected from levothyroxine treatment?

- A. Decreased fatigue.
- B. Better mood.
- C. Improvements in quality of life.
- D. No benefits are expected.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — No benefits are expected.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The BMJ Rapid Recommendation advises **against** levothyroxine for most adults with subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH <20), as trials show **no improvement** in symptoms, mood, fatigue, or quality of life. (Exceptions: TSH ≥20, pregnancy/conception, very young adults.)
