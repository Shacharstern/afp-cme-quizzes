## Q1 — Atrial Fibrillation: Common Questions (p. 398)
A 70-year-old with hypertension has intermittent palpitations for 3 weeks; ECG shows AF, BP normal, pulse 80–90. Which one of the following is an appropriate next step?

- A. Emergent cardioversion.
- B. Calculating the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score to determine the risk of stroke.
- C. Transesophageal echocardiography.
- D. Starting an antiarrhythmic medication.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Calculate the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> A hemodynamically stable patient with AF of unknown/>48 h duration needs stroke-risk stratification to guide anticoagulation. Emergent cardioversion is for instability; TEE is only needed if early cardioversion is planned (to exclude LA thrombus); rate/rhythm drugs come after risk assessment.

## Q2 — Atrial Fibrillation (p. 398)
Which one of the following oral anticoagulants is most appropriate to reduce stroke risk in a patient with AF and moderate mitral stenosis?

- A. Warfarin.
- B. Dabigatran (Pradaxa).
- C. Rivaroxaban (Xarelto).
- D. Apixaban (Eliquis).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Warfarin.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Moderate-to-severe (rheumatic) mitral stenosis defines "valvular AF," for which DOACs are **not** approved — a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) is required. DOACs are preferred only in non-valvular AF.

## Q3 — Depression: Managing Resistance and Partial Response (p. 410)
A patient with diabetes and kidney impairment has had only partial response to adequate doses of two serotonergic antidepressants and is willing to add a second medication to his SNRI. No suicidal ideation. Which one of the following is best?

- A. Second-generation antipsychotic.
- B. Liothyronine (Cytomel).
- C. Ketamine.
- D. Electroconvulsive therapy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Second-generation antipsychotic (augmentation).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> For partial response, adding an SGA (e.g., aripiprazole, quetiapine) has the strongest augmentation evidence. Liothyronine is an alternative; ketamine and ECT are reserved for severe/treatment-resistant or suicidal cases (he is neither). Lithium would be limited by his kidney impairment. **⚠️ Verify** vs liothyronine.

## Q4 — Depression: Managing Resistance (p. 410)
After months of a first-line antidepressant plus psychotherapy, a 25-year-old has no improvement and is currently suicidal. He refuses augmentation pharmacotherapy. Which one of the following should be recommended?

- A. Cannabis.
- B. Dietary changes implemented in the SMILES trial.
- C. Electroconvulsive therapy.
- D. Implanted vagal nerve stimulation.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Electroconvulsive therapy.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> ECT is indicated for severe, treatment-resistant depression with acute suicidality and has the fastest, most robust response. Cannabis lacks evidence/may worsen depression; SMILES dietary changes are adjunctive; implanted VNS is a slow, last-line option, not for acute suicidality.

## Q5 — Evaluation and Treatment of Nausea and Vomiting in Adults (p. 417)
A 28-year-old woman has several days of nausea/vomiting; her child recently had similar symptoms treated with ondansetron. She has slight fever, dry mucous membranes, no abdominal tenderness. Most appropriate next step?

- A. A diagnostic workup, including stool testing.
- B. Start a course of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.
- C. Treat empirically with a serotonin antagonist.
- D. Opioids are recommended as first-line treatment.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Empiric serotonin antagonist (e.g., ondansetron).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The picture is acute viral gastroenteritis with a sick contact — self-limited and treated symptomatically. Routine stool testing isn't warranted without alarm features; NSAIDs aren't antiemetics; opioids are not a treatment for nausea/vomiting.

## Q6 — Nausea and Vomiting in Adults (p. 417)
A 35-year-old with a family history of migraine has recurrent nausea episodes, each with concurrent unilateral throbbing headache; exam unremarkable, pregnancy test negative. Appropriate next step?

- A. A diagnosis of chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome.
- B. Prompt esophagogastroduodenoscopy.
- C. A trial of dopamine antagonists.
- D. A trial of doxylamine/pyridoxine (Bonjesta, Diclegis).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — A trial of dopamine antagonists.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Nausea tied to unilateral throbbing headaches with a migraine family history is migraine-associated nausea; dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) treat both. It isn't "chronic N/V syndrome" because there's an identifiable cause; EGD isn't indicated without alarm features; doxylamine/pyridoxine is for pregnancy.

## Q7 — Substance Misuse in Adults: A Primary Care Approach (p. 430)
Which one of the following statements about screening for substance use is correct?

- A. The AAFP recommends screening all adults for opioid use disorder at every visit.
- B. The USPSTF found insufficient evidence to recommend screening for unhealthy drug use.
- C. The USPSTF recommends screening adults for unhealthy alcohol use.
- D. Alcohol screening and brief intervention can identify unhealthy drinking but have not been shown to reduce use.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — The USPSTF recommends screening adults for unhealthy alcohol use.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Alcohol screening + brief intervention in adults is a Grade B recommendation and *does* reduce risky use (D wrong). The USPSTF now recommends screening adults ≥18 for unhealthy drug use when services are available (B outdated). No body recommends OUD screening "at every visit" (A wrong).

## Q8 — Substance Misuse in Adults (p. 430)
A 31-year-old requests treatment for previously diagnosed alcohol use disorder. Which one of the following is a first-line agent?

- A. Ketamine.
- B. Extended-release intramuscular naltrexone (Vivitrol).
- C. Buprenorphine.
- D. Psilocybin.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Extended-release IM naltrexone (Vivitrol).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Naltrexone (oral or long-acting IM) and acamprosate are first-line for AUD. Buprenorphine treats opioid use disorder; ketamine and psilocybin are not approved AUD therapies.

## Q9 — Acute Pericarditis: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 441)
Which one of the following tests should be included in the initial evaluation of acute pericarditis?

- A. Blood culture.
- B. Antinuclear antibody testing.
- C. Tuberculosis testing.
- D. Transthoracic echocardiography.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Transthoracic echocardiography.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Initial workup is ECG, troponin, inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR), chest radiograph, and TTE (to detect effusion/tamponade). Blood cultures, ANA, and TB testing are obtained only when specific causes are suspected.

## Q10 — Acute Pericarditis (p. 441)
Which one of the following is considered first-line therapy for acute pericarditis?

- A. A nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) and colchicine (Colcrys).
- B. An NSAID and a glucocorticoid, such as prednisone.
- C. Colchicine and a glucocorticoid.
- D. An NSAID, colchicine, and a glucocorticoid.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — NSAID + colchicine.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> NSAID plus colchicine is first-line and colchicine reduces recurrence. Glucocorticoids are second-line (reserved for contraindications/refractory disease or specific etiologies) because they increase recurrence risk.

## Q11 — Falls in Older Adults: Approach and Prevention (p. 447)
At a wellness exam, your healthy 86-year-old reports no past falls and no unsteadiness but says he sometimes worries about falling. Most appropriate next step in evaluating fall risk?

- A. No further evaluation; reevaluate annually.
- B. Perform timed up and go, 30-second chair stand, and four-stage balance tests.
- C. Refer to physical therapy.
- D. Recommend an assistive device.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Perform the gait/strength/balance tests (TUG, 30-sec chair stand, 4-stage balance).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> The CDC STEADI screen asks three questions: prior fall, feeling unsteady, and worry about falling. A "yes" to *any* (here, worry) prompts the functional assessment battery. Referral or assistive devices follow only if those tests reveal deficits. **⚠️ Verify** (some read isolated worry as low risk).

## Q12 — Falls in Older Adults (p. 447)
An older patient with mild cognitive impairment fell, has right groin pain and tenderness, but plain radiography shows no fracture. You remain concerned for fracture. Most appropriate next step?

- A. Ice pack and nonopioid analgesics as needed.
- B. Arrange a home safety evaluation.
- C. Obtain computed tomography to rule out pelvis fracture.
- D. Repeat radiography in 10 days if pain persists.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Obtain CT to rule out an occult pelvis/hip fracture.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Persistent hip/groin pain with tenderness after a fall and normal plain films should prompt advanced imaging for an occult fracture; CT (or MRI, the most sensitive) is appropriate. Waiting 10 days delays a potentially unstable fracture. **⚠️ Verify** (MRI is the most sensitive but isn't an option).

## Q13 — Cochrane: Saw Palmetto for LUTS Due to BPH (p. 396)

- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer

## Q16 — Implementing AHRQ Reviews: Pharmacotherapy for Adults With AUD (p. 459)
Which one of the following statements about pharmacotherapy for alcohol use disorder in adults is correct?

- A. Oral naltrexone (Revia) reduces mortality and lowers the risk of accident and injury.
- B. Acamprosate is contraindicated in patients with liver disease.
- C. Studies have not shown that either oral naltrexone or acamprosate is superior.
- D. Disulfiram is recommended to reduce alcohol consumption–related outcomes.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Neither oral naltrexone nor acamprosate has been shown superior to the other.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Both are effective first-line agents with comparable benefit on drinking outcomes. Naltrexone's mortality/injury benefit is not established (A overstated); acamprosate is actually *preferred* in hepatic disease while naltrexone is avoided (B reversed); disulfiram lacks strong supporting evidence and isn't first-line (D wrong).

## Q17 — Practice Guidelines: Evaluation of Mild TBI in Adults — ACEP (p. 480)
A 71-year-old on a direct-acting oral anticoagulant (for prior VTE) hit her head in a fall, no loss of consciousness, moderate headache, normal neurologic exam. Which one of the following does ACEP recommend?

- A. Head CT now and routine office follow-up if negative.
- B. Head CT now and observation with repeat head CT in 6 hours.
- C. Head CT and 24-hour admission before repeat head CT.
- D. No imaging and follow-up at 1 week.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Head CT now; routine follow-up if negative.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Anticoagulation mandates an initial noncontrast head CT in mild TBI. ACEP found the risk of delayed intracranial hemorrhage after a *negative* initial CT to be low, so routine mandatory observation/repeat CT or admission is not required. **⚠️ Verify** (repeat-CT-in-6 h is the tempting distractor).
