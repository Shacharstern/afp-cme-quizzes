## Q1 — Functional Dyspepsia: Evaluation and Management (p. 84)

Which one of the following statements about the diagnosis of functional dyspepsia is correct?

- A. It is a rare cause of dyspepsia.
- B. A definitive diagnosis can be made using the Rome IV diagnostic criteria in patients who have normal endoscopy results.
- C. Endoscopy should be performed in all patients after two weeks of symptoms.
- D. History is helpful in differentiating between functional and organic dyspepsia.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Rome IV criteria + normal endoscopy establish the diagnosis.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Functional dyspepsia is the **most common** cause of dyspepsia (A wrong). History **cannot reliably** separate functional from organic disease (D wrong); endoscopy is reserved for **alarm features or age ≥60**, not all patients at 2 weeks (C wrong).

## Q2 — Functional Dyspepsia (p. 84)

A 44-year-old patient with six months of dyspepsia symptoms and no concerning signs presents to your office. Which one of the following is the best initial treatment strategy?

- A. Perform an upper endoscopy.
- B. Counsel her to avoid alcohol intake.
- C. Test and treat for Helicobacter pylori infection.
- D. Prescribe a proton pump inhibitor.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Test and treat for H. pylori.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> In patients **<60 without alarm features**, the first step is a **test-and-treat** strategy for H. pylori. A PPI trial follows if symptoms persist (D); endoscopy is for alarm features/older age (A).

## Q3 — Functional Dyspepsia (p. 84)

Which one of the following statements about treating patients with functional dyspepsia is correct?

- A. Patients who continue to have symptoms after treatment of H. pylori infection should be started on a proton pump inhibitor.
- B. Prokinetics are considered first-line therapy for functional dyspepsia.
- C. If acid suppression is ineffective, patients should undergo a trial of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for up to six months.
- D. There is no role for behavior therapy in functional dyspepsia.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Start a PPI if symptoms persist after H. pylori treatment.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The sequence is test-and-treat → **PPI** → then a **tricyclic** antidepressant (not an SSRI, C wrong). PPIs, not prokinetics, are first-line drug therapy (B wrong); **psychotherapy can help** (D wrong).

## Q4 — Short-Term Systemic Corticosteroids: Appropriate Use in Primary Care (p. 89)

Which one of the following conditions has the best evidence to support the treatment benefit of a short course of oral corticosteroids?

- A. Plantar fasciitis.
- B. Allergic rhinitis.
- C. Facial nerve palsy.
- D. Lumbar radiculopathy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Facial nerve palsy (Bell palsy).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Early oral corticosteroids clearly improve recovery in **Bell palsy**. Evidence is weak/absent for short oral steroids in plantar fasciitis, allergic rhinitis (intranasal preferred), and lumbar radiculopathy.

## Q5 — Short-Term Systemic Corticosteroids (p. 89)

Which one of the following statements about the use of oral corticosteroids to treat herpes zoster is correct?

- A. Corticosteroids create more adverse effects when used with antivirals.
- B. Corticosteroids reduce the likelihood of postherpetic neuralgia.
- C. Corticosteroids minimize the risk of secondary bacterial skin infection.
- D. Routine use of corticosteroids is not supported by evidence.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Routine corticosteroid use for herpes zoster is not supported by evidence.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Steroids do **not prevent postherpetic neuralgia** (B wrong) and offer no clear benefit for routine zoster — antivirals are the mainstay.

## Q6 — Muscle Weakness in Adults: Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis (p. 95)

Which one of the following commonly used antibiotic classes is a well-known cause of muscle weakness and can cause rhabdomyolysis?

- A. Aminoglycosides.
- B. Fluoroquinolones.
- C. Macrolides.
- D. Third-generation cephalosporins.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Fluoroquinolones.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Fluoroquinolones cause myalgia/myopathy, tendinopathy/rupture, and can precipitate **rhabdomyolysis** (and worsen myasthenia gravis).

## Q7 — Muscle Weakness in Adults (p. 95)

You are evaluating a patient who has recently developed acute muscle weakness in his left arm. The weakness is asymmetric, occurring on the patient's left side. Which one of the following pathologic processes is most likely to be the cause?

- A. Envenomation (e.g., tick bite paralysis).
- B. Infection.
- C. Inflammatory myositis.
- D. Neurologic event.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — A neurologic event.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> **Asymmetric/focal** weakness points to a neurologic (e.g., stroke, nerve/root) cause. Toxins, infections, and inflammatory myopathies typically produce **symmetric** weakness.

## Q8 — Muscle Weakness in Adults (p. 95)

Which one of the following suggests sarcopenia and frailty in an older patient?

- A. Gottron papules.
- B. High score on the Medical Research Council Manual Muscle Testing scale.
- C. Poor performance on a Timed Up and Go test.
- D. Weakness predominant in the lower extremities.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Poor performance on the Timed Up and Go test.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Slow Timed Up and Go reflects reduced strength/mobility — a marker of sarcopenia/frailty. Gottron papules indicate **dermatomyositis** (A); a high MRC score is **normal strength** (B); proximal (not predominantly lower-limb) weakness suggests myopathy (D).

## Q9 — Putting Prevention into Practice: Screening for Hepatitis B Virus in Pregnant Women (p. 115)

A 33-year-old U.S.-born pregnant clinician comes for a first prenatal visit. She screened negative for HBV more than two years ago, received an HBV vaccination one year ago, and has no substance use. Based on the USPSTF recommendation statement, should you offer this patient HBV screening?

- A. Yes, because she works in a high-risk environment.
- B. Yes, because this is a first prenatal visit.
- C. No, because she had a negative hepatitis B surface antigen test result during her first pregnancy.
- D. No, because she received an HBV vaccination.
- E. No, because she does not have any risk factors for HBV.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Yes — screen all pregnant women at the first prenatal visit.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The USPSTF gives an **A** recommendation to screen **every** pregnant woman with HBsAg at the first prenatal visit — regardless of prior testing, vaccination, or apparent low risk (C, D, E wrong).

## Q10 — Practice Guidelines: Management of Atrial Fibrillation — AHA/ACC/HRS (p. 123)

According to the ACC/AHA/HRS guidelines, which one of the following patients with atrial fibrillation is receiving the recommended treatment?

- A. A 50-year-old woman with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2 being treated with edoxaban (Savaysa).
- B. An 85-year-old man with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2 being treated with apixaban (Eliquis).
- C. A 49-year-old man with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 1 being treated with dabigatran (Pradaxa).
- D. A 74-year-old woman with a mechanical heart valve being treated with rivaroxaban (Xarelto).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — The 85-year-old man (score 2) on apixaba is correctly anticoagulated.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A male score ≥2 warrants a **DOAC**. The 50-year-old woman's score of 2 is **driven solely by female sex** (true risk ~score 1) → anticoagulation not clearly indicated (A); a score of 1 is borderline/optional (C); **mechanical valves require warfarin, not a DOAC** (D contraindicated).
