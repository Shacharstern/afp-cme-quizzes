## Q1 — Eating Disorders in Primary Care (p. 22)

The parents of a 15-year-old girl are concerned about her weight loss. Over six months her BMI has fallen from the 50th to the 10th percentile. She feels she is "too fat," reports an intense fear of gaining weight, and has been skipping breakfast and restricting intake. She denies vomiting or laxative use. Which one of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Binge-eating disorder.
- B. Anorexia nervosa.
- C. Bulimia nervosa.
- D. Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Anorexia nervosa.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The triad of **restriction → low weight, intense fear of weight gain, and body-image distortion** defines anorexia nervosa (restricting type, no purging here). ARFID lacks the weight/shape concern (D); bulimia and binge-eating involve recurrent binges (A, C).

## Q2 — Eating Disorders in Primary Care (p. 22)

You suspect your 16-year-old patient has an eating disorder. Which one of the following would you include in your initial laboratory work up?

- A. Basic metabolic panel.
- B. Liver function test.
- C. Vitamin D level.
- D. Urinalysis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Basic metabolic panel.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A BMP screens for the dangerous **electrolyte and renal derangements** (hypokalemia, hyponatremia) of restriction/purging that drive arrhythmia risk. The others are not part of the core initial workup.

## Q3 — Eating Disorders in Primary Care (p. 22)

Which one of the following behavioral interventions is a first-line treatment for most adolescents with anorexia nervosa?

- A. Cognitive behavior therapy.
- B. Self-guided treatment.
- C. Specialist supportive clinical management.
- D. Family-based therapy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Family-based therapy (FBT).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> FBT (the Maudsley approach) — empowering parents to take charge of refeeding — has the strongest evidence in **adolescent** anorexia. CBT and SSCM are more adult-oriented options.

## Q4 — Breast Cancer Screening: Common Questions and Answers (p. 33)

Which one of the following statements best reflects the USPSTF and AAFP recommendations about breast self-examination for early detection of breast cancer in women at average risk?

- A. Clinicians should teach proper breast self-examination techniques to patients.
- B. Breast self-examinations are only recommended for patients younger than 70 years of age.
- C. Most patients with breast cancer detected the cancer themselves during a breast self-examination.
- D. Breast self-examinations are not recommended.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Breast self-examinations are not recommended.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The USPSTF recommends **against teaching BSE** (no mortality benefit, more false-positives/biopsies). This differs from breast self-*awareness*. Options A–C contradict the guidance.

## Q5 — Breast Cancer Screening (p. 33)

Which one of the following statements is consistent with the USPSTF guidelines on routine mammography screening for breast cancer in women at average risk who are in their 40s?

- A. Screening mammography reduces the rate of advanced breast cancer in this age group, although false-positives are more common.
- B. Screening mammography should begin at 45 years of age.
- C. The number of breast cancer deaths prevented over a 10-year period is higher in this age group than among women in their 50s.
- D. There is no strong evidence supporting mammography screening in this population.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — There is no strong evidence supporting routine mammography in women in their 40s.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> For ages 40–49 the USPSTF gives a **C** recommendation — net benefit is small and the decision should be individualized — which the article frames as weak/insufficient evidence for *routine* screening. Fewer deaths are prevented than in the 50s (C wrong); USPSTF starts at 50 for routine screening, not 45 (B is the ACS position); A overstates the advanced-cancer benefit. *(Teaching nuance: A is partially defensible, but the official key and the article's USPSTF framing select D.)*

## Q6 — Breast Cancer Screening (p. 33)

Your 29-year-old patient was treated for lymphoma at 10 years of age with chest radiotherapy and is considered cured. She has never had a mammogram and her exam is normal. Which one of the following statements about breast cancer screening for this patient is correct?

- A. She should begin screening mammography now.
- B. She should begin screening mammography at 30 years of age.
- C. She should begin screening mammography at 40 years of age.
- D. She should be screened with ultrasonography instead of mammography starting at 40 years of age.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Begin screening mammography at 30 years of age.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Childhood/adolescent **chest radiation** is a strong risk factor; screening (mammography ± MRI) starts at **age 30** (or 8 years after radiation, whichever is later). Waiting until 40 is too late for this high-risk survivor (C, D wrong); the recommended start age is 30, not "now" at 29 (A).

## Q7 — Diagnostic Imaging: Appropriate and Safe Use (p. 42)

Low back imaging should be ordered in which one of the following clinical scenarios?

- A. Four weeks of low back pain described as intermittent, sharp, and 9/10.
- B. Three weeks of low back pain described as continuous, aching, and 6/10.
- C. Seven weeks of low back pain that has not responded to appropriate physical therapy.
- D. Eight weeks of low back pain that improved with physical therapy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Seven weeks of pain not responding to appropriate therapy.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Image only after **≥6 weeks** of pain that fails conservative care (or with red flags). Pain severity alone (A) and shorter durations (B) don't justify imaging; improving pain (D) certainly doesn't.

## Q8 — Diagnostic Imaging (p. 42)

Which one of the following statements about the risks of radiation exposure with imaging is correct?

- A. The estimated risk of developing a fatal malignancy from cumulative radiation exposure is not known.
- B. Lifetime relative risk of developing a fatal malignancy from one CT scan is estimated at one in 2,000.
- C. Estimated lifetime relative risk of developing cancer is 10% per sievert of radiation exposure.
- D. The risk of radiation-associated cancer is higher in adults than children.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — ~1 in 2,000 lifetime risk of fatal malignancy from a single CT.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A commonly cited estimate is ≈1/2,000 per CT. The risk is estimable (A wrong), ≈5% per sievert (C wrong), and **higher in children** because of greater radiosensitivity and longer life expectancy (D reversed).

## Q9 — Diagnostic Imaging (p. 42)

Which one of the following imaging studies is appropriate based on the clinical scenario?

- A. Plain chest radiography during the preoperative assessment of an asymptomatic 64-year-old.
- B. Noncontrast head CT in a 52-year-old with a thunderclap headache.
- C. Abdominal CT with IV contrast as the first study in a 10-year-old with suspected appendicitis.
- D. Neck CT with IV contrast for a 26-year-old with an incidentally discovered thyroid nodule <1 cm.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Noncontrast head CT for thunderclap headache.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Thunderclap headache → urgent **noncontrast head CT** for subarachnoid hemorrhage. Routine preop CXR in an asymptomatic patient is low-value (A); **ultrasound first** for pediatric appendicitis (C); subcentimeter incidental thyroid nodules need no CT (D).

## Q10 — Cochrane for Clinicians: Medications for Treatment-Resistant Depression in Adults (p. 16)

Your patient's depression is inadequately controlled with fluoxetine (Prozac) monotherapy despite dose increases. Which one of the following statements about augmentation with a second antidepressant or atypical antipsychotic is correct?

- A. Adding another antidepressant such as mirtazapine (Remeron) has been shown to improve depression scores.
- B. Adding buspirone (Buspar) can improve depression scores compared with placebo.
- C. Adding an atypical antipsychotic such as quetiapine (Seroquel) can improve depression scores.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Adding an atypical antipsychotic (e.g., quetiapine) can improve depression scores.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The Cochrane review found benefit for **atypical antipsychotic augmentation**, but **not** for adding mirtazapine (A) or buspirone (B) over placebo — so "all of the above" is wrong (D).

## Q11 — Treatment of Distal DVT (p. 18)

Your patient is diagnosed with a distal DVT that does not extend above the knee. Which one of the following statements about warfarin (Coumadin) therapy in this patient is correct?

- A. Twelve weeks of therapy is more effective than six weeks.
- B. It increases the risk of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding.
- C. It reduces the risk of recurrence compared with no anticoagulation or placebo.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Anticoagulating distal DVT reduces recurrence (C) but adds bleeding risk (B), and a longer (12-week) course outperforms a shorter (6-week) one for recurrence prevention (A) — capturing the risk-benefit trade-off.

## Q12 — Practice Guidelines: Testosterone Therapy for Age-Related Low Testosterone — ACP (p. 60)

An 80-year-old has fatigue, low libido, mild cognitive impairment, and erectile dysfunction, with a testosterone of 290 ng/mL and no cause for hypogonadism other than age. According to the ACP, in which one of the following areas can he expect a small improvement with testosterone therapy?

- A. Cognitive function.
- B. Physical function.
- C. Erectile function.
- D. Mood.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Erectile (sexual) function.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The ACP found only a **small improvement in sexual/erectile function**; testosterone did **not** meaningfully improve energy/physical function, cognition, or mood. Clinicians should reassess after ~12 months and continue only if sexual function improves.
