## Q1 — Outpatient Burn Care: Prevention and Treatment (p. 463)

A 13-year-old boy presents with a 1.5-cm, oval scald burn on his left anterior forearm. It is exudative, blanches with pressure, and is painful. Which one of the following statements is correct?

- A. The wound should be debrided and dressed.
- B. Deroofing blisters leads to better outcomes than aspirating them.
- C. Silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene) will hasten wound healing.
- D. He should receive a prophylactic antibiotic.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — The wound should be debrided and dressed.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> This is a superficial partial-thickness burn — clean/debride devitalized tissue and apply a dressing. Evidence doesn't favor deroofing over aspiration (B); **silver sulfadiazine can delay healing** and isn't recommended (C); prophylactic antibiotics aren't indicated for minor burns (D).

## Q2 — Outpatient Burn Care (p. 463)

Burn injuries are dynamic, and even minor-appearing injuries can worsen. Which one of the following can lessen progression of a burn injury?

- A. Leaving clothing on.
- B. Keeping the room environment as cool as possible.
- C. Cooling the burn surface with tap water for 20 minutes.
- D. Using ice to cool the wound.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Cool the burn with tap water for ~20 minutes.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Running cool (not cold) water limits the zone of injury. **Remove** clothing/jewelry (A); avoid systemic hypothermia and **never use ice**, which causes vasoconstriction and deeper injury (B, D).

## Q3 — Outpatient Burn Care (p. 463)

Which one of the following statements about burn aftercare is correct?

- A. Fever within the first 24 hours warrants inpatient treatment.
- B. Direct sun exposure speeds healing.
- C. Dry air can alleviate pruritus.
- D. Patients with burn wounds that do not heal within two weeks should be referred to a burn specialist.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Refer burns that don't heal within two weeks to a burn specialist.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Non-healing at 2–3 weeks suggests deeper injury needing specialist care/grafting. Early low-grade fever is common and not automatically admission-worthy (A); **sun exposure worsens scarring** (B); moisturization, not dry air, relieves pruritus (C).

## Q4 — Chronic Diarrhea in Adults (p. 472)

Which one of the following patients meets Rome IV criteria for irritable bowel syndrome?

- A. A patient who has recurrent abdominal pain with onset three months ago that is related to defecation and a change in the frequency of stool.
- B. A patient who has recurrent abdominal pain with onset three months ago that is not related to defecation or a change in the frequency of stool.
- C. A patient who has recurrent abdominal pain with onset more than six months ago that is related to defecation and a change in the frequency of stool.
- D. A patient who has recurrent abdominal pain with onset more than six months ago that is related to defecation and not to a change in the frequency of stool.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Onset >6 months ago, pain related to defecation and a change in stool frequency.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Rome IV requires symptom onset **≥6 months** before diagnosis, with recurrent pain ≥1 day/week in the last 3 months associated with defecation and/or a change in stool **frequency or form** — option C alone fits.

## Q5 — Chronic Diarrhea in Adults (p. 472)

Which one of the following is an alarm feature that warrants further investigation in a patient with chronic diarrhea?

- A. Unintentional weight loss.
- B. Elevated hematocrit level.
- C. Low level of C-reactive protein.
- D. Decreased appetite.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Unintentional weight loss.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Red flags include weight loss, **anemia** (not high hematocrit, B), nocturnal/bloody diarrhea, **elevated** inflammatory markers (not low CRP, C), and a family history of IBD/cancer. Decreased appetite alone is nonspecific (D).

## Q6 — Chronic Diarrhea in Adults (p. 472)

Which one of the following statements about the categories of chronic diarrhea (watery, fatty, and inflammatory) is correct?

- A. Division into a category should always be based on analysis of 24-hour stool mass, quantitative fecal leukocytes, and fecal chemistry.
- B. Watery diarrhea typically contains leukocytes, blood, and fat.
- C. Fatty diarrhea includes malabsorptive and maldigestive types.
- D. Inflammatory diarrhea does not contain fecal leukocytes or blood.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Fatty diarrhea includes malabsorptive and maldigestive types.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Steatorrhea results from **malabsorption** (e.g., celiac) or **maldigestion** (e.g., pancreatic insufficiency). Categorization is usually clinical, not always full stool studies (A); **inflammatory** (not watery) diarrhea contains leukocytes/blood (B, D reversed).

## Q7 — Fetal Aneuploidy: Screening and Diagnostic Testing (p. 481)

Which one of the following groups should be offered screening for fetal aneuploidy?

- A. Pregnant women 30 years and older.
- B. Pregnant women 40 years and older.
- C. Pregnant women with a family history of trisomy 21.
- D. All pregnant women.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All pregnant women.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> ACOG recommends that **all** pregnant women, regardless of age or risk, be **offered** both aneuploidy screening and diagnostic testing options.

## Q8 — Fetal Aneuploidy (p. 481)

Which one of the following is the definition of positive predictive value?

- A. The likelihood that a positive test result reflects a true positive.
- B. The likelihood that a positive test result reflects a false positive.
- C. The percentage of individuals with a condition correctly identified as positive for that condition.
- D. The percentage of individuals without a condition incorrectly identified as positive for that condition.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — The likelihood that a positive test result reflects a true positive.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> PPV = true positives ÷ all positives. Option C describes **sensitivity**, and D describes the **false-positive rate** — important because even high-sensitivity cell-free DNA screening has a modest PPV in low-prevalence (low-risk) populations.

## Q9 — Putting Prevention into Practice: Screening for Asymptomatic Bacteriuria in Adults (p. 493)

A 33-year-old woman presents for a first prenatal visit at 8 weeks' gestation with morning nausea and breast tenderness; the exam is consistent with early pregnancy and otherwise unremarkable. Based on the USPSTF recommendation statement, which one of the following is most appropriate?

- A. Do not screen for asymptomatic bacteriuria because the patient has no symptoms.
- B. Obtain a urine dipstick screening test from the patient today.
- C. Obtain a urine culture from the patient today.
- D. Obtain a urinalysis with microscopic analysis from the patient today.
- E. Defer screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria until the third trimester.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Obtain a urine culture today.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> The USPSTF recommends screening pregnant women for ASB with a **urine culture** (the gold standard) early in pregnancy (~12–16 weeks or first visit). Dipstick/urinalysis are insufficiently sensitive (B, D); screening is **indicated despite no symptoms** (A, E wrong).

## Q10 — Point-of-Care Guides: Predicting the Likelihood of a Recurrent Febrile Seizure (p. 497)

The presence of which one of the following predicts an increased risk of febrile seizure recurrence?

- A. Age older than 18 months.
- B. Fever lasting more than one hour.
- C. Family history of febrile seizure.
- D. Temperature higher than 104°F (40°C) at the time of the seizure.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — A family history of febrile seizures.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Recurrence risk factors: **young age (<18 months)**, family history, **lower** peak temperature, and **short duration of fever before** the seizure — so A, B, and D (older age, long fever, high temperature) actually indicate *lower* recurrence risk.

## Q11 — Practice Guidelines: Acute Altitude Illness — Wilderness Medical Society (p. 505)

While trekking above 12,000 ft (3,650 m), a hiker presents with headache, vomiting, fatigue, and confusion. According to the Wilderness Medical Society, which one of the following treatments is most likely to be beneficial?

- A. Acetazolamide, 250 mg orally.
- B. Dexamethasone, 8 mg intramuscularly.
- C. Supplemental oxygen by face mask at a rate of 12 L per minute.
- D. Immediate descent of 1,000 to 3,300 ft (300 to 1,000 m).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Immediate descent of 1,000–3,300 ft.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Confusion/ataxia indicate **high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE)** — the definitive treatment is **immediate descent**. Dexamethasone and oxygen are useful adjuncts, and acetazolamide is for prevention/AMS, but descent is the priority and most beneficial (A, B, C secondary).
