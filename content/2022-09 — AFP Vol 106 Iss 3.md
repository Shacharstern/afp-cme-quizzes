## Q1 — Evidence-Based Contraception (p. 251)
A 24-year-old with obesity and hypertension, no desire for pregnancy, requests emergency contraception within 48 hours. Most effective EC?

- A. Four COC pills (ethinyl estradiol 0.03 mg + levonorgestrel 0.15 mg).
- B. Copper intrauterine device (Paragard).
- C. Oral levonorgestrel 1.5 mg (Plan B One-Step).
- D. Oral ulipristal (Ella).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Copper IUD (Paragard).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The copper IUD is the most effective EC (>99%) and, unlike oral options, is not less effective with higher body weight. Levonorgestrel efficacy falls with obesity; ulipristal is the best *oral* choice but still less effective than the IUD.

## Q2 — Evidence-Based Contraception (p. 251)
Which LARC method has had its FDA-certified use period extended?

- A. Copper IUD (Paragard) for 11 years.
- B. Depo-subQ Provera every 120 days.
- C. Etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon) for five years.
- D. Levonorgestrel IUS 52 mg (Mirena) for seven years.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Mirena 52 mg for seven years.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> FDA extended Mirena's labeled duration (to 7 yr, later 8 yr). Paragard remains 10 yr; Nexplanon FDA-labeled 3 yr (5-yr data are off-label); Depo-subQ is every 12–13 weeks. Answer confirmed; the exact year figure was being revised around this period.

## Q4 — Acute Headache in Adults (p. 260)
Which statement about neuroimaging in acute headache is correct?

- A. New-onset headaches in adults 20–40 years should prompt neuroimaging.
- B. Newly diagnosed migraine or tension-type headache needs baseline neuroimaging.
- C. Stable primary headache disorders with no red flags do not need neuroimaging.
- D. Non-contrast brain MRI is first-line for emergent headache evaluation.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Stable primary headaches without red flags need no imaging.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Primary headaches with a reassuring history/exam do not warrant imaging. In the *emergent* setting, non-contrast **CT** (not MRI) is first-line (e.g., to detect SAH).

## Q7 — Malaria (p. 270)
A 26-year-old has intermittent fevers, returned 4 weeks ago from India. Which fever pattern should prompt malaria evaluation?

- A. Fever daily since returning.
- B. Fever every second day for the past week.
- C. Fever every third day for the past two weeks.
- D. All of the above — any fever after travel to an endemic region warrants evaluation.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Classic tertian/quartan periodicity is unreliable; ANY fever in a returning traveler from an endemic area must be evaluated for malaria.

## Q8 — Malaria (p. 270)
Which statement about rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) for malaria in the U.S. is correct?

- A. They may be used alone to diagnose malaria.
- B. They have excellent sensitivity and negative predictive value.
- C. They are useful for monitoring response to treatment.
- D. They provide information regarding parasite density.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Excellent sensitivity and negative predictive value.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> RDTs cannot stand alone — a positive (and a clinically suspicious negative) must be confirmed by microscopy, which is what provides species and parasite density and monitors treatment. RDTs are not used for monitoring. Answer confirmed; B is the only true statement.

## Q9 — Malaria (p. 270)
Which clinical sign should prompt treatment for *severe* malaria?

- A. Hyperglycemia.
- B. Alkalosis.
- C. Severe anemia (Hgb <7 g/dL adults; <5 g/dL children <12 y).
- D. Parasite density less than 5%.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Severe anemia.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Severe malaria criteria include severe anemia, *hypo*glycemia (not hyper), metabolic *acidosis* (not alkalosis), and parasitemia *>5%*.

## Q10 — Thrombocytopenia (p. 288)
A 68-year-old, platelet 126 ×10³/μL, normal WBC/RBC, recent travel + COVID booster, minor bruise, no bleeding. Appropriate next step?

- A. Coagulation panel (d-dimer, fibrinogen, factors).
- B. Viral serologies (hepatitis, HIV).
- C. Repeat platelet count with blood in heparin or sodium citrate tube.
- D. Testing for autoimmune syndromes and antiplatelet antibodies.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Repeat count in heparin/citrate tube.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> First exclude **pseudothrombocytopenia** from EDTA-induced platelet clumping by repeating in a citrate/heparin tube (and reviewing the smear) before launching a workup.

## Q11 — Thrombocytopenia (p. 288)
Most common cause of isolated thrombocytopenia (no systemic illness, normal RBC/WBC)?

- A. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia.
- B. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.
- C. Immune thrombocytopenia.
- D. Hepatosplenomegaly.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion and the most common cause of isolated thrombocytopenia in otherwise-well patients.

## Q14 — Chronic Constipation in Adults (p. 299)

## Q17 — Tuberculosis (p. 308)

## Q18 — Tuberculosis (p. 308)
A 42-year-old who immigrated from sub-Saharan Africa 6 months ago, asymptomatic, positive IGRA at work entry. Appropriate next step?

- A. Collect three sputum specimens.
- B. Order chest radiography and, if negative, begin isoniazid plus rifapentine (Priftin).
- C. Order chest CT.
- D. Monitor and treat only if symptoms develop.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Chest x-ray; if negative, treat LTBI with isoniazid + rifapentine (3HP).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> A positive IGRA needs a CXR to exclude active TB before treating latent infection. 3HP (weekly INH + rifapentine ×12) is a preferred short-course LTBI regimen.

## Q23 — Point-of-Care Guides: Diagnosis of UTI in Women (p. 335)
Which one is NOT a predictor of UTI in the Dutch history-and-dipstick risk score?

- C. Vaginal irritation.

## Q24 — Practice Guidelines: Predicting/Preventing Preterm Birth (ACOG) (p. 337)
At 20 weeks, endovaginal cervical length 20 mm, no dilation, (singleton, no prior preterm birth implied). Most effective treatment to reduce preterm birth risk?

- A. Cervical cerclage.
- B. Cervical pessary.
- C. Intramuscular progesterone.
- D. Vaginal progesterone.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Vaginal progesterone.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> For an incidentally short cervix without prior spontaneous preterm birth, vaginal progesterone reduces preterm birth. Cerclage is reserved for short cervix *with* a prior preterm birth; pessary is not effective; IM progesterone is for prior preterm birth, not short cervix alone.
