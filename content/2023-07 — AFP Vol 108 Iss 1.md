## Q1 — Managing Menopausal Symptoms (p. 28)
Which statement about hormone therapy for a postmenopausal patient is correct?

- A. Progesterone monotherapy is recommended for vasomotor symptoms.
- B. Compounded preparations are preferred over FDA-approved formulations.
- C. Transdermal combinations of estrogen and progesterone are more effective than placebo.
- D. Patients with a uterus should not use hormone therapy for vasomotor symptoms.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Transdermal estrogen-progesterone combinations are more effective than placebo.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Combined estrogen-progestogen therapy effectively treats vasomotor symptoms. Progesterone alone isn't recommended for VMS, compounded "bioidenticals" are **not** preferred over FDA-approved products, and women **with** a uterus can use HT — they just need a progestogen for endometrial protection.

## Q2 — Managing Menopausal Symptoms (p. 28)
A postmenopausal woman, intact uterus, no breast-cancer history, with vaginal dryness/irritation. Which statement about treatment is correct?

- A. A vaginal lubricant is recommended on an as-needed basis.
- B. Estradiol vaginal cream, 10 mcg, is more effective than nonhormonal therapy.
- C. Treatment with vaginal estrogen will require endometrial protection.
- D. Ospemifene (Osphena) is the recommended treatment for decreased libido.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — A vaginal lubricant on an as-needed basis.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Nonhormonal moisturizers/lubricants are first-line for genitourinary symptoms. **Low-dose vaginal estrogen does NOT require systemic progestogen/endometrial protection** (C wrong); ospemifene is for moderate-severe dyspareunia, not libido (D wrong).

## Q3 — Childhood Eye Examination in Primary Care (p. 40)
Which statement about instrument-based vision screening (photoscreening, autorefraction) in children is correct?

- A. It is more difficult to use than vision charts in uncooperative older children.
- B. It has a higher positive predictive value than conventional vision-chart screening.
- C. It should not be used until at least five years of age.
- D. The AAP recommends against instrument-based vision screening.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — It has a higher positive predictive value than conventional chart screening.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Instrument-based screening is especially useful in **young or preverbal/uncooperative** children (easier, not harder), is endorsed by the AAP, and can be used well before age 5.

## Q4 — Childhood Eye Examination (p. 40)
A 2-year-old with several months of one eye deviating inward; cover-uncover confirms esotropia. Most common cause of acquired esotropia?

- A. Amblyopia.
- B. Cataract.
- C. Farsightedness.
- D. Increased light sensitivity.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Farsightedness (hyperopia).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> **Accommodative esotropia** from uncorrected hyperopia is the most common acquired esotropia: the child over-accommodates to focus, driving convergence. (Amblyopia is a *consequence* of, not the cause of, the misalignment.)

## Q5 — RSV Bronchiolitis: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 52)
For which child is palivizumab prophylaxis recommended?

- A. A 2-year-old with a neuromuscular disorder.
- B. A 15-month-old with congenital heart disease.
- C. An 11-month-old born at 28 weeks of gestation.
- D. A 3-year-old who is immunocompromised.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — An 11-month-old born at 28 weeks' gestation.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Palivizumab is indicated for infants born **<29 weeks** during their **first year** of life. The 11-month-old (still <12 months, born at 28 weeks) qualifies. CHD/CLD indications also generally apply only in the first year (so the 15-month-old doesn't qualify), and there's no routine indication for the others.

## Q6 — RSV Bronchiolitis (p. 52)
Which is recommended for treatment of RSV in children?

- A. Nebulized hypertonic saline.
- B. Corticosteroids.
- C. Antibiotics.
- D. Supplemental oxygen to maintain saturation above 90%.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Supplemental oxygen to keep saturation above 90%.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Bronchiolitis management is **supportive** (oxygen, hydration). Hypertonic saline, corticosteroids, bronchodilators, and antibiotics are **not** routinely recommended.

## Q7 — Fatigue in Adults (p. 58)
A 38-year-old diagnosed with ME/CFS. Which treatment is likely to be **harmful**?

- A. Education about pacing.
- B. Tricyclic antidepressant for sleep difficulty and pain.
- C. Graded exercise therapy.
- D. Referral to a pain management specialist.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Graded exercise therapy.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> In ME/CFS, graded exercise therapy can trigger **post-exertional malaise** and worsen the illness; current guidance (NICE 2021) advises against it. Management centers on **pacing** / activity management.

## Q8 — Fatigue in Adults (p. 58)
Which treatment has **no** evidence supporting it for secondary fatigue?

- A. Pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD.
- B. Pharmacologic treatment targeting fatigue related to chronic disease.
- C. Tai chi for fibromyalgia.
- D. Acupuncture for patients with cancer.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Pharmacologic treatment targeting chronic-disease-related fatigue.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Fatigue secondary to chronic disease is best addressed with **non-drug**, disease-directed strategies (pulmonary rehab, tai chi, acupuncture all have some supportive evidence). Drugs aimed directly at the fatigue symptom lack evidence.

## Q9 — Ischemic Stroke: Posthospitalization & Transition of Care (p. 70)
A 58-year-old after a mild stroke (NIHSS 2), started on aspirin + clopidogrel, anxious about bleeding (mother died of hemorrhagic stroke). What is the **shortest** interval of dual antiplatelet therapy shown to reduce recurrent stroke?

- A. 3 days.
- B. 10 days.
- C. 30 days.
- D. 90 days.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — 10 days.** ✅ Official (key letter) · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> The benefit of short-course DAPT after minor stroke/high-risk TIA is concentrated early, so a brief course captures most of the recurrent-stroke reduction while limiting bleeding. ⚠️ Major trials used 21 days (CHANCE) or 90 days (POINT, with early benefit); the precise "shortest shown effective" figure is article-specific — confirm the 10-day value against the print PDF.

## Q10 — Ischemic Stroke (p. 70)
A 64-year-old, 3 months post-stroke, stopped all meds; BP 140/90, LDL 110. Recommended secondary-prevention therapy?

- A. CCB to lower BP to 130/80.
- B. Atorvastatin 80 mg/day to LDL <100.
- C. ACE inhibitor to BP 135/85 + rosuvastatin 20 mg to LDL <100.
- D. ARB to BP 130/80 + atorvastatin 80 mg to LDL <70.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — ARB to BP <130/80 plus high-intensity atorvastatin to LDL <70.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Secondary stroke prevention targets **BP <130/80** and an **LDL <70** with a high-intensity statin. The other options under-treat one or both targets.

## Q11 — Mpox: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 78)
Transmission of monkeypox virus occurs **primarily** through which route?

- A. Respiratory droplets.
- B. Vaginal intercourse.
- C. Anal intercourse.
- D. Skin lesions.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Skin lesions (direct contact).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Mpox spreads mainly by **direct contact with infectious skin lesions**. Sexual contact is a common *context* for that skin-to-skin transmission, but the primary mode is lesion contact, not a specific intercourse route or respiratory droplets.

## Q12 — Mpox (p. 78)
Which statement about treatment of mpox is correct?

- A. Tecovirimat (Tpoxx) is first-line for healthy, immunocompetent patients.
- B. Treatment is primarily supportive for most patients.
- C. Trials support tecovirimat, cidofovir, and brincidofovir in patients with mild symptoms.
- D. Tecovirimat causes serious adverse effects including stroke and VTE.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Treatment is primarily supportive for most patients.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Most mpox is self-limited and managed supportively. Antivirals (tecovirimat) are reserved for severe disease or high-risk/immunocompromised patients, not routine first-line therapy.

## Q13 — Cochrane: Biomarkers to Guide Antibiotics in ARI (p. 24)
Which statement about using CRP to guide antibiotic prescribing in acute respiratory infections is correct?

- A. Use of CRP changes clinical recovery rates.
- B. Use of CRP changes hospital admission rates.
- C. Use of CRP reduces antibiotic prescribing.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — CRP point-of-care testing reduces antibiotic prescribing.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> CRP-guided prescribing safely **lowers antibiotic use** without changing clinical recovery or hospital-admission rates — so "all of the above" is wrong.

## Q14 — Cochrane: Conservative Interventions for Urinary Incontinence (p. 25)
Which conservative intervention is an effective treatment option?

- A. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) for all types of UI.
- B. Vaginal cones for stress incontinence.
- C. Electrical stimulation for stress incontinence.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> PFMT (first-line, effective across UI types), vaginal cones, and electrical stimulation all have supporting evidence as conservative options for stress/urinary incontinence.

## Q15 — Practice Guidelines: Headache in Pregnancy/Breastfeeding (ACOG) (p. 97)
A 25-year-old, 10 weeks pregnant, two severe migraines in 3 weeks, wants an abortive option. Per ACOG, most appropriate **oral** medication?

- A. Nifedipine, 30 mg.
- B. Acetaminophen, 1,000 mg, with 130 mg caffeine.
- C. Naproxen, 500 mg.
- D. Sumatriptan, 50 mg.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Acetaminophen 1,000 mg with 130 mg caffeine.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Acetaminophen (± caffeine) is the first-line abortive for migraine in pregnancy. NSAIDs like naproxen are avoided (especially after 20 weeks), triptans are not first-line, and nifedipine isn't a migraine abortive.

## Q16 — Practice Guidelines: Osteoporosis Treatment (ACOG) (p. 100)
A 66-year-old with obesity, DEXA T-score −2.5, **PE 6 months ago** (recently stopped anticoagulation), no prior fracture, low fracture-risk score (2%). Per ACOG, most appropriate treatment?

- A. Alendronate, a bisphosphonate.
- B. Raloxifene, a SERM.
- C. Romosozumab, a sclerostin-binding analog.
- D. Teriparatide, a parathyroid hormone analog.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Alendronate (a bisphosphonate).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> A bisphosphonate is first-line for osteoporosis. **Raloxifene is contraindicated** here — it increases VTE risk and she has a recent PE. Romosozumab and teriparatide are reserved for very-high-risk patients (and romosozumab carries cardiovascular cautions).
