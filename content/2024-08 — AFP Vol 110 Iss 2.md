## Q1 — Conjunctivitis: Diagnosis and Management (p. 134)
A 38-year-old has conjunctival erythema and watery discharge that started in one eye, now both; her daughter has similar symptoms. Most appropriate recommendation?

- A. Polymerase chain reaction testing to identify the pathogen.
- B. Cold compresses to relieve symptoms.
- C. Topical antibiotics to prevent secondary bacterial infection.
- D. Steroid monotherapy to decrease viral shedding.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Cold compresses for symptomatic relief.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Bilateral watery discharge with a sick contact is viral conjunctivitis — self-limited, managed with supportive care (cold compresses, artificial tears). PCR isn't routinely needed, prophylactic antibiotics don't help, and topical steroids are avoided (can worsen herpetic disease).

## Q2 — Conjunctivitis (p. 134)
A 10-year-old with allergic rhinitis and dry eye has red, watery, itchy eyes, no pain/photophobia/vision change. Most likely helpful approach?

- A. Measurement of serum immunoglobulin E (IgE).
- B. Treatment with topical antibiotics.
- C. A trial of topical antihistamines with mast cell–stabilizing activity.
- D. Treatment with oral antihistamines.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Topical antihistamine/mast-cell stabilizer.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The picture is allergic conjunctivitis; dual-action topical antihistamine/mast-cell stabilizers (e.g., olopatadine, ketotifen) are first-line. Serum IgE doesn't guide management, antibiotics are unnecessary, and oral antihistamines are less effective for ocular symptoms (and can worsen dry eye).

## Q5 — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder in Adults (p. 157)
In which patient with ADHD would a stimulant prescription be most appropriate?

- A. A 30-year-old pregnant woman at 24 weeks taking aspirin for preeclampsia prevention.
- B. A 23-year-old former athlete with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy taking propranolol.
- C. A 35-year-old treated for moderate major depression on bupropion, ineffective for concentration.
- D. A 27-year-old with a history of alcohol and opiate abuse taking naltrexone.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — The patient with MDD whose bupropion hasn't helped concentration.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Stimulants are reasonable to add here. They're avoided in pregnancy (A), contraindicated in structural heart disease/HCM (B), and used cautiously given the misuse/diversion risk in active substance-use history (D).

## Q6 — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder in Adults (p. 157)
A 24-year-old graduate student with poor concentration and distractibility wonders if he has ADHD. Which symptom is consistent with the diagnostic criteria?

- A. Initial onset of symptoms at 13 years of age.
- B. Symptoms in no more than one sphere of life.
- C. Symptoms have a significant impact on function.
- D. Three or more symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity from DSM-5-TR.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Symptoms significantly impair function.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> DSM-5-TR requires functional impairment plus onset of several symptoms before age 12 (so 13 is too late — A wrong), symptoms in ≥2 settings (B wrong), and ≥5 symptoms in adults/≥6 in children (so "three or more" is insufficient — D wrong).

## Q7 — Chronic Cough: Evaluation and Management (p. 167)
Which one of the following is the least common cause of chronic cough in adults?

- A. Upper airway cough syndrome.
- B. Asthma.
- C. Nonasthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis.
- D. Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The common ("big three/four") causes of chronic cough are upper airway cough syndrome, asthma, GERD, and nonasthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis. ILD/pulmonary fibrosis is a comparatively uncommon cause.

## Q8 — Chronic Cough (p. 167)
A 43-year-old with obesity has chronic cough you attribute to GERD. Reasonable first step in evaluation/management?

- A. Counseling on smoking cessation, head-of-bed elevation, and avoiding food triggers and late meals.
- B. Monitoring pH levels.
- C. Performing esophagogastroduodenoscopy.
- D. Starting a 1-week trial of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Lifestyle counseling (weight, head elevation, trigger/late-meal avoidance).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Empiric lifestyle modification is the reasonable first step for suspected GERD-related cough. pH monitoring and EGD are reserved for refractory/alarm cases, and an effective PPI trial runs ~1–2 months, not 1 week (so D's duration is wrong). **⚠️ Verify** (an empiric PPI trial is the close alternative if lifestyle alone isn't the keyed answer).

## Q9 — Mastitis: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 174)
Which one of the following statements about the diagnosis of mastitis is correct?

- A. Mastitis is almost always caused by a bacterial infection and requires antibiotics.
- B. WBC count, differential, and CRP should be obtained to distinguish bacterial from inflammatory causes.
- C. Lactational mastitis usually occurs within 3 months postpartum but may present in the first 2 to 3 weeks postpartum.
- D. Occasionally missing feedings is a risk factor for mastitis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Lactational mastitis usually occurs within ~3 months postpartum, often the first 2–3 weeks.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> Mastitis is frequently inflammatory rather than infectious (A wrong) and is a clinical diagnosis not requiring routine bloodwork (B wrong). Updated (ABM) thinking attributes mastitis to hyperlactation/over-emptying rather than "missed feedings," so D is no longer considered accurate. **⚠️ Verify.**

## Q10 — Mastitis (p. 174)
Which one of the following statements about the treatment of mastitis is correct?

- A. Heat application and massaging the breast are helpful with milder episodes.
- B. Frequent breast pumping to empty the breast is helpful.
- C. Antibiotics should be started within 24 hours of diagnosis.
- D. If a bacterial infection is suspected, dicloxacillin is a first-line antibiotic.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Dicloxacillin is a first-line antibiotic when bacterial infection is suspected.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Updated guidance favors cold compresses and gentle handling over heat/deep massage (A wrong) and warns that aggressive pumping/over-emptying worsens inflammation (B wrong); antibiotics aren't started by a 24-hour clock but for persistent/worsening or clearly bacterial disease (C wrong). Anti-staphylococcal agents (dicloxacillin, cephalexin) are first-line.

## Q11 — Pulmonary Hypertension (p. 183)
A patient with HFpEF and moderate aortic stenosis has worsening exertional dyspnea. Which symptom most reliably distinguishes pulmonary hypertension from heart failure and valvular disease?

- A. Dyspnea.
- B. Fatigue.
- C. Syncope.
- D. None of these findings.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — None of these findings.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> The symptoms of pulmonary hypertension (dyspnea, fatigue, exertional syncope) are nonspecific and overlap heavily with heart failure and valvular disease, so none reliably distinguishes PH clinically — diagnosis requires echocardiography and right heart catheterization. **⚠️ Verify.**

## Q12 — Pulmonary Hypertension (p. 183)
A 62-year-old with COPD (60 pack-years) has worsening exertional dyspnea; echo shows pulmonary hypertension confirmed by right heart catheterization. Recommended first-line therapy?

- A. A phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor, such as tadalafil.
- B. A high-dose calcium channel blocker, such as nifedipine.
- C. An anticoagulant, such as apixaban.
- D. A smoking cessation program and optimizing COPD treatment.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Smoking cessation and optimizing COPD treatment.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> This is Group 3 PH (due to lung disease/hypoxia). Management targets the underlying disease — smoking cessation, optimized COPD therapy, and oxygen. PAH-specific drugs (PDE-5 inhibitors, high-dose CCBs) and routine anticoagulation are not first-line and may be harmful in Group 3 PH.

## Q13 — Cochrane: Blood Pressure Control for Patients With Diabetic Retinopathy (p. 131)

## Q14 — Cochrane: Diagnosing Dementia and Cognitive Impairment in Symptomatic Patients (p. 132)
Which one of the following statements about diagnosing cognitive impairment/dementia is correct?

- A. PCP judgment is sensitive, but not specific, for diagnosing cognitive impairment in symptomatic patients.
- B. Primary care physicians should not rely solely on clinical judgment when evaluating for dementia or cognitive impairment.
- C. The USPSTF recommends against screening for cognitive impairment in asymptomatic, community-dwelling people ≥65 years.

> [!success]- Reveal answer

## Q16 — Practice Guidelines: Resistant Bacterial Infections (IDSA) (p. 207)
A 52-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and dysuria grows 100,000 CFU *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* resistant to piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin. Per IDSA, which medication is recommended?

- A. Ceftolozane/tazobactam (Zerbaxa).
- B. Nitrofurantoin.
- C. Ciprofloxacin.
- D. Tobramycin.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Ceftolozane/tazobactam.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> This is difficult-to-treat-resistant (DTR) *Pseudomonas*. IDSA recommends a newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor such as ceftolozane/tazobactam (or ceftazidime/avibactam, imipenem/relebactam). The isolate is already resistant to ciprofloxacin, and nitrofurantoin lacks *Pseudomonas* activity; aminoglycosides are not preferred first-line.
