## Q1 — Acute Abdominal Pain in Adults (p. 585)
An 85-year-old man with 2 hours of constant abdominal pain. Which statement is correct?

- A. Cholelithiasis is less likely than in a younger patient.
- B. If the examination doesn't suggest an obvious cause, consider CT angiography to look for mesenteric ischemia.
- C. Imaging is less important than in younger patients with a similar presentation.
- D. A lack of rebound tenderness and guarding can be used to rule out peritonitis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Consider CT angiography for mesenteric ischemia.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> In older adults, "pain out of proportion to exam" should prompt evaluation for **mesenteric ischemia**. Imaging is *more* important in the elderly (atypical presentations), gallstones are *more* common with age, and absent peritoneal signs do **not** exclude peritonitis in this group.

## Q2 — Acute Abdominal Pain (p. 585)
A 55-year-old with LLQ pain suspected to be diverticulitis. For which finding would CT be **required**?

- A. Diarrhea without visible blood.
- B. No previous episodes of diverticulitis.
- C. Rebound tenderness in the LLQ.
- D. Symptoms for the past three days.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Rebound tenderness in the LLQ.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Peritoneal signs (rebound) raise concern for **complicated** diverticulitis (abscess, perforation), mandating CT. Uncomplicated cases can often be managed on clinical grounds without imaging.

## Q3 — Growth Faltering and Failure to Thrive (p. 597)
Most common cause of growth faltering (failure to thrive) in young children?

- A. Autism spectrum disorder.
- B. Excessive use of energy.
- C. Inadequate absorption of nutrients.
- D. Inadequate caloric intake.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Inadequate caloric intake.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> By far the most common mechanism is **insufficient calories in** (inadequate intake), not malabsorption or excessive expenditure. Organic disease accounts for a small minority of cases.

## Q4 — Growth Faltering (p. 597)
An otherwise healthy child whose z-score fell from −1.4 (15 mo) to −1.7 (18 mo). Recommended intervention?

- A. Completion of a food diary.
- B. Increased intake of fruit juices.
- C. Vitamin D supplementation.
- D. Appetite stimulant.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Completion of a food diary.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Mild growth faltering in a well child is first evaluated with a **dietary/feeding assessment** (food diary). Fruit juice is discouraged (displaces calories, causes diarrhea), and appetite stimulants/vitamin D aren't indicated here.

## Q6 — COPD (p. 604)
A 74-year-old, 3 hospitalizations for exacerbations this year, already on LAMA/LABA/ICS. Which add-on most reduces further exacerbations?

- A. Azithromycin, a macrolide.
- B. Dextromethorphan, an antitussive.
- C. N-acetylcysteine, a mucolytic.
- D. Prednisone, an oral corticosteroid.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Azithromycin.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> In patients with frequent exacerbations despite optimal inhaler therapy, **chronic azithromycin** (or roflumilast) reduces exacerbation frequency. Chronic oral prednisone is harmful and not recommended; antitussives/mucolytics don't prevent exacerbations meaningfully.

## Q7 — Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 613)

## Q11 — Common Types of Supraventricular Tachycardia (p. 631)
A 17-year-old with palpitations and near-syncope, anxiety history, normal vitals; ECG shows regular P waves with slurring of the QRS complex. Most likely diagnosis?

- A. Panic attack.
- B. Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.
- C. Sepsis.
- D. Atrial fibrillation.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> "Slurring of the QRS complex" is a **delta wave** — the hallmark of ventricular pre-excitation via an accessory pathway (WPW). Don't anchor on the anxiety history.

## Q12 — Supraventricular Tachycardia (p. 631)
A hemodynamically **stable** patient with undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia despite vagal maneuvers. Reasonable next step?

- A. Perform immediate cardioversion.
- B. Refer immediately for catheter ablation.
- C. Use adenosine diagnostically.
- D. Start propafenone to stop the arrhythmia.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Use adenosine diagnostically.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> In a **stable** patient with a regular wide-complex tachycardia, adenosine can be both diagnostic and therapeutic (it terminates/unmasks SVT with aberrancy). Immediate cardioversion is for instability; ablation isn't an acute step; propafenone isn't appropriate first-line here.

## Q14 — Systemic Corticosteroids for Low Back Pain (p. 583)
Which statement about systemic corticosteroids in low back pain is correct?

- A. In radicular pain, they improve short-term function.
- B. In nonradicular pain, they improve short-term function.
- C. In spinal stenosis, they improve short-term function.
- D. A short course of corticosteroids is associated with significant adverse effects.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — In radicular pain, they improve short-term function.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Systemic steroids offer modest **short-term functional** improvement in **radicular** pain (sciatica) but not in nonradicular pain or spinal stenosis; short courses are generally not associated with significant adverse effects.

## Q15 — Putting Prevention Into Practice: USPSTF Hormone Therapy (p. 645)
Per the USPSTF recommendation on hormone therapy for primary prevention of chronic conditions in postmenopausal people, which risk is **increased** by hormone therapy?

- A. All-cause mortality.
- B. Colorectal cancer.
- C. Urinary incontinence.
- D. Diabetes mellitus.
- E. Fractures.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Urinary incontinence.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Systemic HT **increases** urinary incontinence (along with stroke, VTE, gallbladder disease, dementia). It **decreases** fractures, colorectal cancer, and diabetes — which is precisely why the USPSTF recommends **against** using HT for chronic-disease prevention (harms outweigh benefits).

## Q17 — Lown Right Care: Overdiagnosis of CKD in Older Adults (p. 657)
Which statement about CKD in older adults is correct?

- A. Approximately one-half of adults older than 75 meet CKD criteria based on a decreased eGFR.
- B. Adults >65 with eGFR 45–59 and no/mild albuminuria have a higher risk of kidney failure and death than those without CKD.
- C. Patients with CKD account for less than 10% of Medicare spending.
- D. Most older adults with CKD die from complications of end-stage kidney disease.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — About half of adults older than 75 meet CKD criteria by decreased eGFR.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> This is the crux of the overdiagnosis argument: applying a fixed eGFR <60 threshold labels roughly half of people >75 as having "CKD," much of which reflects normal age-related decline. Those with mild reduction and no albuminuria are **not** at meaningfully higher risk (B false), CKD drives a **large** share of Medicare spending (C false), and most die of cardiovascular disease, not ESKD (D false).

## Q18 — Practice Guidelines: Neonatal Hyperbilirubinemia (AAP) (p. 661)
A 39-week infant discharging at 37 h after uncomplicated cesarean; mother A-positive, Rh-antibody negative; feeding well. TcB at 35 h was 8.7 mg/dL, below the 14.7 mg/dL phototherapy threshold. Per AAP, which measurement is recommended?

- A. G6PD activity.
- B. Serum bilirubin immediately.
- C. Serum bilirubin in 4–8 hours and delay discharge until results return.
- D. Transcutaneous or serum bilirubin in two days.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Transcutaneous or serum bilirubin in two days.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The 2022 AAP guideline sets follow-up timing by how far the bilirubin sits below the phototherapy threshold. With a comfortable ~6 mg/dL margin (8.7 vs 14.7) and no risk factors, a **follow-up bilirubin in ~2 days** is appropriate — no need to delay discharge, draw immediately, or test G6PD.
