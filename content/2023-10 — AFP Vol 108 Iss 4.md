## Q1 — Hypertension in Adults (p. 352)
A 54-year-old man has a confirmed office blood pressure of 148/92 mm Hg on repeated measurements. He has no diabetes, no chronic kidney disease, and a 10-year ASCVD risk of 7%. What is the most appropriate initial step?

- A. Begin a thiazide diuretic immediately
- B. Recommend lifestyle modification and reassess in 3–6 months
- C. Begin an ACE inhibitor immediately
- D. Begin dual therapy with a thiazide and an ACE inhibitor

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Recommend lifestyle modification and reassess in 3–6 months.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> For stage 1 hypertension (130–139/80–89) — and even low-stage 2 in a patient with ASCVD risk <10% and no compelling indication — guideline-concordant care begins with lifestyle modification (DASH diet, sodium restriction, weight loss, exercise, alcohol moderation) before pharmacotherapy. Drug therapy is reserved for ASCVD ≥10%, diabetes, CKD, or BP ≥140/90 persisting after lifestyle trial.

## Q3 — Over-the-Counter Medications in Pregnancy (p. 360)

- D. Ibuprofen

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Loratadine.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: Medium ⚠️

## Q4 — Over-the-Counter Medications in Pregnancy (p. 360)
Which of the following should be discouraged in a pregnant patient seeking an OTC remedy?

- A. Acetaminophen for pain
- B. Calcium carbonate for heartburn
- C. Topical/oral retinoid-containing acne products
- D. Bulk-forming laxatives for constipation

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Topical/oral retinoid-containing acne products.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Retinoids (including topical adapalene/tretinoin and especially oral isotretinoin) are teratogenic and contraindicated. Acetaminophen, calcium carbonate antacids, and bulk-forming laxatives are all considered acceptable in pregnancy.

## Q5 — Chronic Rhinosinusitis (p. 370)
A 45-year-old has 14 weeks of nasal congestion, facial pressure, and purulent drainage. Endoscopy reveals nasal polyps. What is the most appropriate management consideration?

- A. A 10-day course of amoxicillin-clavulanate
- B. Oral antihistamine monotherapy
- C. Intranasal corticosteroid and referral to otolaryngology
- D. Reassurance, no treatment needed

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Intranasal corticosteroid and referral to otolaryngology.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Chronic rhinosinusitis (≥12 weeks) is an inflammatory, not primarily infectious, disease. First-line is intranasal corticosteroids ± saline irrigation; the presence of nasal polyps warrants ENT referral for evaluation and consideration of endoscopic surgery or biologics. Antibiotics are not first-line for the chronic form.

## Q6 — Chronic Rhinosinusitis (p. 370)
Which intervention has the strongest evidence as a cornerstone of daily symptom management in chronic rhinosinusitis?

- C. Chronic systemic antibiotics
- D. Daily oral corticosteroids

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — High-volume saline nasal irrigation plus intranasal corticosteroid.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High

## Q7 — Cryptorchidism (p. 378)
A healthy 9-month-old boy has a unilateral nonpalpable testis that has not descended. What is the recommended next step?

- A. Refer to a pediatric surgeon/urologist for orchidopexy
- B. Reassure and observe until age 2 years
- C. Begin a trial of hCG hormonal therapy
- D. Obtain abdominal ultrasound to localize the testis

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Refer to a pediatric surgeon/urologist for orchidopexy.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Spontaneous descent is unlikely after 6 months. Guidelines recommend referral so orchidopexy can be performed by 6–18 months (ideally by 12 months) to reduce risks of infertility and malignancy. Imaging is NOT recommended for a nonpalpable testis (poor sensitivity/specificity); hormonal therapy is not routinely recommended.

## Q8 — Cryptorchidism (p. 378)
What counseling point should be given to the family regarding long-term risk after treated cryptorchidism?

- A. There is no increased cancer risk after orchidopexy
- B. Fertility is unaffected regardless of treatment timing
- C. There remains an increased risk of testicular cancer, so self-examination is advised after puberty
- D. Prophylactic orchiectomy is recommended in adulthood

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — There remains an increased risk of testicular cancer, so self-examination is advised after puberty.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> A history of cryptorchidism carries a lifelong elevated testicular cancer risk that orchidopexy reduces but does not eliminate; orchidopexy also makes the testis examinable. Patients should be taught testicular self-examination starting in adolescence.

## Q9 — Nontraumatic Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (p. 386)
A 48-year-old presents with a sudden "worst headache of my life" peaking within seconds. A noncontrast head CT within 6 hours is the best initial test. If positive, what finding is expected?

- A. Hyperdense blood within the basal/subarachnoid cisterns
- B. A hypodense wedge-shaped cortical infarct
- C. Diffuse cerebral edema with effaced sulci only
- D. A hyperdense MCA sign

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Hyperdense blood within the basal/subarachnoid cisterns.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Acute subarachnoid blood appears hyperdense, tracking into the basal cisterns and sulci. Noncontrast CT within 6 hours of onset approaches ~100% sensitivity; beyond that window, a negative CT should be followed by lumbar puncture (xanthochromia) or CT angiography.

## Q10 — Nontraumatic Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (p. 386)
Which clinical decision tool helps identify which alert patients with acute nontraumatic headache warrant investigation for SAH?

- A. The Ottawa SAH Rule
- B. The Wells score
- C. The PERC rule
- D. The CHADS-VASc score

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — The Ottawa SAH Rule.** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> The Ottawa SAH Rule applies to alert patients ≥15 years with a new severe nontraumatic headache peaking within 1 hour; it is highly sensitive for ruling out SAH. Wells/PERC are for VTE, CHADS-VASc for AF stroke risk.

## Q11 — The Ill Returning Traveler (p. 396)
What is the single most important symptom or sign that mandates urgent evaluation in a febrile traveler returning from sub-Saharan Africa?

- A. Mild diarrhea
- B. A maculopapular rash
- C. Fever (consider malaria until proven otherwise)
- D. Fatigue

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Fever (consider malaria until proven otherwise).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> Fever in a traveler returning from a malaria-endemic region is a medical emergency — *Plasmodium falciparum* must be excluded with thick and thin smears (or RDT) urgently, as it can be rapidly fatal. Fever is the cardinal red-flag symptom driving the workup.

## Q12 — The Ill Returning Traveler (p. 396)
What is the most common cause of travelers' diarrhea worldwide?

- A. Norovirus
- B. Enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli* (ETEC)
- C. *Giardia lamblia*
- D. *Entamoeba histolytica*

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli* (ETEC).** 🧠 Generated · Confidence: High
> ETEC is the most frequent bacterial — and overall most common identified — cause of travelers' diarrhea. Most cases are self-limited and managed with hydration; azithromycin is preferred empiric therapy in many regions due to fluoroquinolone resistance.
