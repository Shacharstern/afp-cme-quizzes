## Q1 — Syncope: Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis (p. 454)
44-year-old, "woozy" then 15-second syncope after standing and walking, no abnormal movements or postictal confusion. Recommended evaluation?

- A. Echocardiography.
- B. Carotid ultrasonography.
- C. Prolonged ECG monitoring.
- D. Orthostatic blood pressure measurement.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Orthostatic blood pressure measurement.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Classic orthostatic/reflex syncope (positional trigger, prodrome, rapid recovery, no red flags) → bedside orthostatic vitals. Echo/prolonged ECG are for suspected cardiac syncope; carotid US has no role in syncope workup.

## Q2 — Syncope (p. 454)
Which finding is consistent with orthostatic hypotension on moving supine → standing?

- A. A drop in systolic BP of 23 mm Hg.
- B. A drop in diastolic BP of 7 mm Hg.
- C. An increase in heart rate of five beats per minute.
- D. A decrease in heart rate after three minutes of standing.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — SBP drop of 23 mm Hg.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Orthostatic hypotension = fall in SBP ≥20 mm Hg or DBP ≥10 mm Hg within 3 minutes of standing. A 23 mm Hg systolic drop meets criteria; the 7 mm Hg diastolic drop does not.

## Q3 — Pleural Effusion: Diagnostic Approach in Adults (p. 464)
56-year-old: fever, dyspnea, right pleuritic pain; small-to-moderate right effusion, >2 cm on US without complex features; no CHF/malignancy. Most appropriate next step?

- A. Antibiotics and monitoring.
- B. Thoracentesis with fluid analysis.
- C. Consultation for tPA/DNase therapy.
- D. Consultation for VATS.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Thoracentesis with fluid analysis.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> A new effusion >1 cm of unclear cause warrants diagnostic thoracentesis (Light's criteria, culture). tPA/DNase and VATS are for established complicated/loculated empyema, not the initial step.

## Q4 — Pleural Effusion (p. 464)
Which statement about imaging for pleural effusion is correct?

- A. The PA view detects smaller fluid volumes than the lateral upright view.
- B. Plain radiography can distinguish transudates from exudates.
- C. Pleural fluid attenuation on CT can distinguish transudates from exudates.
- D. POCUS can detect effusions as small as 20 mL and characterize complex effusions.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — POCUS detects ~20 mL and characterizes complex effusions.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Ultrasound is the most sensitive bedside modality and shows septations/loculations. The lateral view detects smaller volumes than PA; neither plain film nor CT attenuation reliably separates transudate from exudate (that needs fluid analysis).

## Q5 — Sodium Disorders (p. 476)
Acute confusion and ataxia, Na 123 mEq/L. Indicated treatment?

- A. Desmopressin (2 mcg) infusion.
- B. 100-mL bolus of normal saline.
- C. 100-mL bolus of 3% hypertonic saline.
- D. Continuous normal saline to raise Na by no more than 3 mEq/L per hour.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — 100-mL bolus of 3% hypertonic saline.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Acute *symptomatic* hyponatremia (neurologic signs) is treated with a 100-mL 3% saline bolus (repeatable ×2), targeting a rise of ~4–6 mEq/L. Correct slowly overall (<8–10 mEq/L per 24 h) to avoid osmotic demyelination.

## Q6 — Sodium Disorders (p. 476)
Most common cause of hypernatremia?

- A. Decreased water intake.
- B. Diabetes insipidus.
- C. Iatrogenic (excess hypertonic saline).
- D. Excessive salt intake.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Decreased water intake.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Hypernatremia is almost always a water-deficit problem — impaired access to/drive for water (elderly, infants, impaired cognition). Pure sodium gain and DI are far less common.

## Q7 — Common Intestinal Parasites (p. 487)
Preferred diagnostic test for suspected Giardia infection?

- A. PCR assay.
- B. Stool microscopy.
- C. Rapid antigen testing.
- D. Microscopy with direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) testing.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — DFA microscopy.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> DFA is the reference (most sensitive/specific) test for Giardia and Cryptosporidium, outperforming conventional O&P microscopy. (PCR/NAAT and antigen EIA are alternatives; the article designates DFA as preferred.)

## Q8 — Common Intestinal Parasites (p. 487)
Recommended treatment for Cyclospora in immunocompetent adults?

- A. Albendazole.
- B. Mebendazole.
- C. Nitazoxanide (Alinia).
- D. Sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — TMP/SMX.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Cyclospora cayetanensis responds to TMP-SMX (first-line). Albendazole/mebendazole target helminths; nitazoxanide is used for Giardia/Cryptosporidium.

## Q9 — Managing Difficult Patient Encounters (p. 494)
Patient who is demanding and quick to anger. Most beneficial strategy?

- A. Assess how to take control of the encounter.
- B. Ignore difficulties and attempt to be interactive.
- C. Recognize strong emotions when they occur.
- D. Set your agenda and then listen to the patient's concerns.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Recognize strong emotions when they occur.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Naming/acknowledging emotion de-escalates and builds alliance. "Taking control" and "setting your agenda first" are power-struggle framings that tend to worsen difficult encounters.

## Q10 — Managing Difficult Patient Encounters (p. 494)
Patient yells "you have no idea what you are talking about." Helpful communication strategy?

- A. "Do you want to get better, or are you satisfied with your current state?"
- B. "Help me to understand why this upsets you so much."
- C. "Would you prefer to see another doctor?"
- D. "Your lack of activity is probably the cause of your pain."

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — "Help me to understand why this upsets you so much."** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> An empathic, curious open question validates emotion and invites the patient's perspective. The others are confrontational, dismissive, or blaming.

## Q11 — Dog and Cat Bites: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 501)
Which statement about antibiotic prophylaxis for a dog or cat bite is correct?

- A. Antibiotics should be given for cat bites, but not for all dog bites.
- B. Underlying medical conditions do not impact the decision.
- C. Clindamycin 300 mg TID plus ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID is preferred first-line for adults.
- D. For dog and cat bites that have not broken the skin, prophylaxis is not warranted.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — No prophylaxis if the skin is not broken.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Intact skin = no infection risk → no antibiotics. Comorbidities (immunocompromise, asplenia) *do* influence the decision; first-line prophylaxis is amoxicillin/clavulanate, not clinda+cipro.

## Q12 — Dog and Cat Bites (p. 501)
Which statement about dog bite wound management is correct?

- A. Tetanus vaccine should be routinely given for all bites.
- B. Wounds at low infection risk or of cosmetic concern may be considered for primary closure.
- C. Rabies PEP is mandatory after any bite by an unknown/unavailable animal.
- D. Wound cultures should be routinely obtained.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Low-risk/cosmetic wounds may be considered for primary closure.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Selected low-risk wounds (especially facial/cosmetic) can be primarily closed after irrigation. Tetanus is given per immunization status (not blanket); rabies PEP depends on risk assessment; cultures aren't routine for fresh wounds.

## Q13 — Cochrane: Environmental Interventions for Preventing Falls (p. 450)
Which intervention, used alone, reduces falls in community-dwelling older adults?

- A. Specialized footwear.
- B. Bed alarm systems.
- C. Patient education.
- D. Programs that assess and address home fall hazards.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Home hazard assessment and modification.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Cochrane evidence supports home-hazard assessment/modification (especially in higher-risk people) for reducing falls. Footwear, bed alarms, and education alone lack convincing fall-reduction evidence.

## Q14 — Cochrane: Class I/III Antiarrhythmics After AF Ablation (p. 451)
Which statement about class I/III antiarrhythmic drugs after catheter ablation of AF is correct?

- A. They reduce hospitalizations in the first three months and AF recurrence at three to six months postablation.
- B. They reduce all-cause mortality.
- C. They reduce thromboembolic events.
- D. They reduce the risk of myocardial infarction.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Fewer early hospitalizations and less AF recurrence at 3–6 months.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Antiarrhythmics during the post-ablation blanking period reduce early hospitalizations and short-term recurrence but show no benefit for mortality, thromboembolism, or MI.

## Q15 — AHRQ: Postpartum Care Up to One Year After Pregnancy (p. 506)
Which statement about postpartum health care delivery is correct?

- A. Home-based care probably reduces depression/anxiety symptoms vs. clinic care.
- B. Earlier contraceptive interventions lead to higher continued IUD use at six months.
- C. More comprehensive insurance probably increases attendance at postpartum visits.
- D. Peer support for breastfeeding does not affect breastfeeding rates in the first six months.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Better insurance coverage probably improves postpartum visit attendance.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> Coverage is a key access driver for postpartum follow-up. Peer support *does* improve breastfeeding rates; the home-care and early-contraception claims were not supported with that certainty.

## Q16 — Practice Guidelines: CDC Prescribing Opioids for Pain (p. 523)
Long-term opioid therapy (18 months) for cervical radiculopathy; constipation and a frightening episode of falling asleep while caregiving. Per the CDC, most effective way to taper?

- A. Taper as quickly as the patient will tolerate.
- B. Reduce the dosage by up to 10% per month.
- C. Reduce the dosage by up to 10% per week.
- D. Switch to buprenorphine, first dose ≥4 hours after the last opioid.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Reduce by up to 10% per month.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> For patients on long-term (>1 year) opioids, the CDC advises a slow taper — on the order of 10% per month — to limit withdrawal and improve success. Faster (10%/week) tapers are reserved for shorter-duration use.

## Q17 — Practice Guidelines: AGA Medications for IBS (p. 527)
Per the AGA, which medication has the most evidence to improve symptoms (including pain) in IBS-C?

- A. Duloxetine (Cymbalta), an SNRI.
- B. Linaclotide (Linzess), a guanylate cyclase-C agonist.
- C. Polyethylene glycol, an osmotic laxative.
- D. Sertraline, an SSRI.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Linaclotide.** ✅ Official · Confidence: High
> The AGA gives a strong recommendation (high-quality evidence) for linaclotide in IBS-C, with benefit for global symptoms and abdominal pain. PEG improves stool frequency but not pain; antidepressants aren't first-line for IBS-C specifically.
