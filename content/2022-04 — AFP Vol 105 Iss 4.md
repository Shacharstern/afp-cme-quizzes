# AFP CME Quiz — April 2022 (Vol 105, Issue 4)

## Q1 — Common Oral Lesions (p. 369)
A 60-year-old man, 40 pack-years, has a 6-month painless white oral plaque, no trauma. Best option?

- A. Performing a biopsy.
- B. Scheduling evaluation twice per year.
- C. Prescribing topical corticosteroids.
- D. Prescribing chlorhexidine (Peridex) rinses.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Performing a biopsy.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Persistent leukoplakia in a heavy smoker is potentially premalignant → biopsy to exclude dysplasia/SCC, don't just monitor or treat empirically.

## Q2 — Common Oral Lesions (p. 369)
Which oral lesion is associated with hepatitis C virus?

- A. Hairy tongue.
- B. Torus.
- C. Geographic tongue.
- D. Lichen planus.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Lichen planus.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Oral lichen planus has a recognized association with HCV; consider HCV testing in patients with oral LP.

## Q3 — Common Oral Lesions (p. 369)
A 30-year-old with first-ever painful crusted oral vesicular lesion (herpes labialis). Most effective treatment?

- A. Topical penciclovir 1%, BID ×7 days.
- B. Valacyclovir, 1 g BID ×10 days.
- C. Acyclovir 5%, topically once daily.
- D. Docosanol 10%, topically twice daily.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Valacyclovir 1 g BID.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Oral antivirals are more effective than topicals for herpes labialis; topical agents (penciclovir, docosanol) offer only marginal benefit.

## Q4 — Recurrent Venous Thromboembolism (p. 377)
Which patient should continue anticoagulation beyond the initial 3 months?

- A. Unprovoked VTE, low bleeding risk.
- B. Unprovoked VTE, high bleeding risk.
- C. Provoked VTE, low bleeding risk.
- D. Provoked VTE, high bleeding risk.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Unprovoked VTE with low bleeding risk.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Unprovoked VTE → high recurrence → extended anticoagulation if bleeding risk is acceptable. Provoked (transient risk factor) → stop at 3 months.

## Q5 — Recurrent VTE / thrombophilia (p. 377)
Which statement about thrombophilia evaluation in VTE is correct?

- A. Guidelines differ about evaluating provoked VTE.
- B. Thrombophilia evaluation should be offered to all unprovoked VTE.
- C. Thrombophilia evaluation may benefit a small subset interested in discontinuing anticoagulation.
- D. Routine thrombophilia evaluation improves VTE mortality.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — May benefit a small subset considering stopping anticoagulation.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Routine testing rarely changes management and doesn't improve outcomes; reserve for select cases.

## Q6 — Chlamydial & Gonococcal Infections (p. 388)
Which statement about screening is correct?

- A. Asymptomatic newborns of an untreated infected birth parent need no screening.
- B. Sexually active patients with a cervix <25 years should be screened annually.
- C. MSM do not require regular screening.
- D. Pregnant patients treated for GC/CT need no additional screening.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Patients with a cervix <25 years: annual screening.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Exposed newborns need evaluation; MSM need regular (often more frequent) screening; treated pregnant patients need test-of-cure and rescreening.

## Q7 — Chlamydial & Gonococcal Infections (p. 388)
Most appropriate treatment for uncomplicated urogenital/anorectal gonorrhea (nonpregnant, nonobese)?

- A. Ceftriaxone 1 g IM daily ×3 days.
- B. Cefixime 800 mg PO single dose.
- C. Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose.
- D. Gentamicin 240 mg IM + azithromycin 2 g PO single dose.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> 2020/2021 CDC monotherapy; add doxycycline only if chlamydia not excluded. Gent+azithro is the alternative for cephalosporin allergy.

## Q8 — Chlamydial & Gonococcal Infections (p. 388)
Appropriate treatment for ophthalmia neonatorum caused by chlamydia?

- A. Erythromycin base/ethyl succinate 50 mg/kg/day in 4 doses PO ×14 days.
- B. Doxycycline 100 mg PO BID ×7 days.
- C. Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily ×7 days.
- D. Amoxicillin 500 mg PO TID ×7 days.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Oral erythromycin 50 mg/kg/day ×14 days.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Systemic (not topical) treatment required; doxycycline/levofloxacin contraindicated in neonates.

## Q9 — Common Sleep Disorders in Adults (p. 397)
A 50-year-old with 6 months of sleep-maintenance insomnia. Most appropriate initial intervention?

- A. Sleep hygiene handout.
- B. Referral for polysomnography.
- C. Cognitive behavior therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).
- D. OTC antihistamines.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — CBT-I.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> CBT-I is first-line for chronic insomnia; sleep hygiene alone is insufficient as monotherapy; avoid antihistamines.

## Q10 — Common Sleep Disorders (p. 397)
Optimal diagnostic approach for restless legs syndrome?

- A. Polysomnography.
- B. A trial of CPAP.
- C. A five-criteria symptom questionnaire (URGED mnemonic).
- D. The STOP-Bang questionnaire.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Five-criteria clinical questionnaire (URGED).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> RLS is a clinical diagnosis: Urge to move, Rest worsens, Gets better with movement, Evening worse, not explained by other condition. STOP-Bang is for OSA.

## Q11 — Common Sleep Disorders / OSA (p. 397)
Apneic episodes + daytime fatigue. Most effective monotherapy demonstrating reduced all-cause mortality?

- A. Mandibular advancement devices.
- B. Long-term positive airway pressure therapy.
- C. Benzodiazepine receptor agonists.
- D. Cognitive behavior therapy.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — Long-term PAP therapy.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> CPAP is the gold standard for OSA; oral appliances are an alternative for mild/intolerant cases.

## Q12 — Ulcerative Colitis: Rapid Evidence Review (p. 406)
Which statement about treating ulcerative proctitis is correct?

- A. First-line therapy is 5-ASA for mild disease.
- B. Oral budesonide is first-line for mild disease.
- C. Fecal microbiota transplantation is routine for mild disease.
- D. Fish oil is recommended for all stages.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — 5-ASA is first-line for mild ulcerative proctitis.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Topical (and/or oral) mesalamine is first-line; FMT/fish oil not standard.

## Q13 — Ulcerative Colitis (p. 406)
Which is a risk factor for ulcerative colitis?

- A. Rural residence.
- B. Tobacco use.
- C. Tea consumption.
- D. Recent Salmonella, C. difficile, or Campylobacter infection.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Recent enteric infection (Salmonella, C. difficile, Campylobacter).** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> 🔑 Tobacco is *protective* in UC (opposite of Crohn's). Urban living increases risk.

## Q14 — Ulcerative Colitis / fecal calprotectin (p. 406)
Which statement about fecal calprotectin in UC diagnosis is correct?

- A. An abnormal test confirms UC.
- B. A negative test virtually rules out IBD in children.
- C. It is not helpful to monitor for relapse.
- D. Useful to rule out UC in adults but not children.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **B — A negative fecal calprotectin virtually rules out IBD in children.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> High NPV; it's a screening/monitoring marker, not confirmatory (elevated in any GI inflammation). Useful for relapse monitoring.

## Q15 — Alcoholic Hepatitis: Diagnosis & Management (p. 412)
Which statement about diagnosis of alcoholic hepatitis is correct?

- A. Liver biopsy should always be performed.
- B. Total bilirubin of 2.5 mg/dL is consistent with alcoholic hepatitis.
- C. Acute-onset jaundice is the only required clinical symptom for diagnosis.
- D. Thirty days of abstinence before jaundice onset is inconsistent with the diagnosis.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **C — Acute-onset jaundice is the cardinal/required clinical symptom.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: Medium ⚠️
> *Answer confirmed by official key; nuance only.* Rapid-onset jaundice (within ~8 weeks of heavy use) is the hallmark; biopsy is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty; criteria require bilirubin >3.0 (so 2.5 is below); abstinence up to ~60 days before jaundice is still compatible.

## Q16 — Alcoholic Hepatitis (p. 412)
Which patient should receive corticosteroids for severe alcoholic hepatitis?

- A. Maddrey DF 20, no infection.
- B. Maddrey DF 33, active infection.
- C. MELD 18, active infection.
- D. MELD 22, no infection.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — MELD 22, no concomitant infection.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Severe = Maddrey DF ≥32 or MELD >20. Active infection contraindicates steroids; DF 20 is not severe.

## Q17 — Alcoholic Hepatitis / AUD pharmacotherapy (p. 412)
Cirrhosis + persistently elevated enzymes, patient wants medication to stop drinking. Most appropriate therapy?

- A. Acamprosate.
- B. Naltrexone (Revia).
- C. Lactulose.
- D. Disulfiram (Antabuse).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Acamprosate.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Acamprosate is renally cleared and safe in liver disease; naltrexone is hepatotoxic and disulfiram is contraindicated in significant liver disease/cirrhosis.

## Q18 — Cochrane: Cilostazol for Intermittent Claudication (p. 366)
Intermittent claudication from PAD. What does AHA/ACC recommend as initial therapy?

- A. Exercise therapy and smoking cessation.
- B. Pentoxifylline (Trental).
- C. Cilostazol (Pletal).
- D. Surgery.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — Exercise therapy and smoking cessation.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Supervised exercise + risk-factor modification is first-line; cilostazol is added if symptoms persist.

## Q19 — Cochrane: Oral vs Intravaginal Antifungals for VVC (p. 367)
Which statement about oral vs intravaginal therapy for uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis is correct?

- A. Clinical resolution is similar with oral and intravaginal therapy.
- B. Unclear whether oral therapy reduces adverse effects vs intravaginal.
- C. CDC guidelines suggest either oral or topical treatment.
- D. All of the above.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — All of the above.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Comparable efficacy; route choice is by patient preference.

## Q20 — Practice Guideline: Adults With Down Syndrome (Global DS Foundation) (p. 436)
A 21-year-old with Down syndrome, unremarkable exam. Which screening test is recommended?

- A. Celiac antibody panel.
- B. Cervical spine radiography.
- C. A1C level.
- D. Thyroid-stimulating hormone.

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **D — Thyroid-stimulating hormone.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Thyroid disease is common in Down syndrome → periodic TSH. Routine celiac serology (asymptomatic) and cervical spine films are NOT recommended without symptoms.

## Q21 — Practice Guideline: Prevention of Type 2 Diabetes (ADA) (p. 438)
A1C 6.4% (prediabetes). Which intervention is most likely to prevent/delay type 2 diabetes per ADA?

- A. Lifestyle intervention based on the National Diabetes Prevention Program.
- B. Initiation of the Mediterranean diet.
- C. Treatment with metformin.
- D. Phentermine/topiramate (Qsymia).

> [!success]- Reveal answer
> **A — National DPP–based lifestyle intervention.** ✅ Official-verified · Confidence: High
> Intensive lifestyle change (≥7% weight loss, 150 min/wk activity) is most effective; metformin is second-line (consider if BMI ≥35, age <60, or prior GDM).
